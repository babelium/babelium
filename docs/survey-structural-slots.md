# Structural Slots: What Sinks Actually Carry, and Where Registers Come From Without Autoregression

This note covers two papers that push past the question of *why* an attention sink forms and ask what it actually *does* once formed: "Attention Sinks: A 'Catch, Tag, Release' Mechanism for Embeddings" (Zhang, Khan, and Papyan, arXiv:2502.00919), which argues a sink's value vector writes a shared, semantically readable tag into every token that attends to it; and "Vision Transformers Need Registers" (Darcet, Oquab, Mairal, and Bojanowski, ICLR 2024, arXiv:2309.16588), which finds an essentially identical repurposed-slot phenomenon in vision transformers with no causal mask, no autoregression, and no language at all. Read together with the four attention-sink papers covered in [Attention Sinks](survey-attention-sinks.md), these two papers supply the piece those four leave open: once a structurally-forced slot exists (whether from a causal mask's position-zero asymmetry, or from a bidirectional ViT's excess representational capacity), what is actually stored there, and is the storage mechanism itself dependent on autoregressive decoding at all. This note was written as background for the token theory's discussion of absent addresses and bolted-on positions ([The Conversion Signature](theory-signature.md), section 2.5), and for the abstention mechanism discussed in [The Mechanism Layer](theory-mechanisms.md), section 3.2.

## 1. Catch, Tag, Release: what a sink's value vector actually does

### 1.1 The mechanism, separated into two sides

This paper's central move is separating a sink's **key-side** behavior (attracting disproportionate attention) from its **value-side** behavior (what actually gets computed once attention lands there). Once a token becomes a sink, its value vector is effectively copied into every later token that attends to it, writing a shared, low-dimensional direction — a **tag** — into those tokens' representations. That tag persists through the residual stream and becomes something later layers can use as a basis for grouping, retrieval, or selecting which tokens to operate on. The paper's own framing for the three stages: a boundary or punctuation-like token **catches** attention from subsequent tokens; its value **tags** those tokens with a shared direction; later layers **release** that tag by using it to identify or aggregate the tagged set.

### 1.2 Evidence the tag carries genuine, decodable content

Using PCA on attention-head outputs, one or two tag directions typically explain 20% to 40% of the variance in head output across a range of models, occasionally as much as 70% — the tag is not a minor perturbation, it is a dominant component of what a downstream head actually sees. More directly, a linear probe trained on the tag direction alone (as opposed to a probe trained on the full token activation) classifies the truth of a factual statement in a cities dataset at 92.5% to 99.5% accuracy across five different models, while a probe explicitly denied access to the tag direction performs close to chance; a parallel sentiment-classification extension reaches 86.5% to 94.0% accuracy under the same tag-only probing setup. Strikingly, the tag-only probe sometimes *outperforms* a probe using the full activation vector, which the authors read as evidence that the tag provides a **denoised** representation of the relevant semantic direction (truth, sentiment) — the tag is not merely a leftover trace of semantic content that happens to be present elsewhere too, it appears to be a cleaner, more linearly accessible copy of it.

**A distinction worth being precise about**: this is evidence that the tag direction can be *read out*, not evidence that the model's original task performance *depends on* this readability. Probing establishes decodability; it does not, by itself, establish necessity for the behavior the model is actually trying to produce.

### 1.3 A minimal, sufficient toy construction, and its explicit limits

To establish that this mechanism is at least *sufficient* to accomplish something useful, the paper hand-constructs the parameters of a two-layer causal transformer solving a toy sequence-averaging task: given a sequence containing a `[SEP]` marker, average only the tokens that follow it. The construction works by exactly the catch-tag-release logic: `[SEP]` catches attention, tags the following tokens with a shared marker via its value vector, and the second layer releases that tag by using it to select which tokens enter the final average. Separately, training (rather than hand-constructing) a model on this same toy task shows the identical strategy can emerge from gradient descent, though only with a training success rate the authors themselves report as no better than 4 out of 10 runs — a real, if modest, existence result, not a claim that this strategy reliably emerges. The authors state directly that their theorem establishes **sufficiency, not necessity**: nothing here rules out an entirely different mechanism accomplishing the same toy task without attention sinks or outlier features at all, and the toy task itself is explicitly flagged as far simpler than real-world language modeling.

### 1.4 An architectural ablation, and a suggestive but unestablished correlation

Comparing models trained with QK normalization (which constrains query and key vector norms directly) against models without it shows the total number of sinks staying roughly similar between the two settings — direct evidence that constraining Q/K norm alone does not eliminate the underlying mechanism that produces sinks in the first place, ruling out one simple architectural fix. Separately, a reasoning-distilled model (a DeepSeek-R1-distilled Qwen variant) shows a higher average sink count (1.125 rising to 2.196) and more attention heads with high explained variance from the tag subspace, compared to its non-distilled base. **This correlation is reported as exactly that — a correlation** — and the paper does not rule out other changes introduced by the distillation process as alternative explanations for the difference; no causal manipulation isolates reasoning-distillation itself as the driver.

### 1.5 Scope: everything here is measured in causal, autoregressive models

Every empirical measurement in this paper — the PCA analysis, the probing experiments, the QK-normalization comparison, the reasoning-distillation comparison — is conducted on causal decoder-only large language models, and the toy theoretical construction explicitly uses causal attention. **This means the paper cannot, on its own evidence, establish whether the catch-tag-release mechanism depends on autoregressive decoding or the causal mask specifically**, as opposed to being a more general property of attention-plus-residual-stream architectures that would appear under bidirectional attention too. The mechanism as described is general in principle — nothing in its logic obviously requires a causal mask — but the paper itself provides no vision-transformer or other non-autoregressive experiment to test that generality directly. This is exactly the gap the second paper in this note fills, from a completely different direction.

## 2. Vision Transformers Need Registers: the same repurposing, with no autoregression and no language at all

### 2.1 The phenomenon: patches that stop being patches

Large, sufficiently trained vision transformers develop a small number of outlier tokens — roughly 2% of patches in DINOv2-g — whose output norm reaches roughly ten times that of an ordinary patch token. These outlier tokens overwhelmingly arise from patches in low-information, locally redundant background regions (areas visually similar to their neighbors, carrying little unique local content). The paper's central interpretive claim: the model has learned to recognize which patches contain little useful local information and **repurposes** the corresponding token slot as an internal scratchpad for aggregating and storing global, image-level information, at the direct cost of discarding that patch's own local spatial and pixel identity.

This phenomenon appears only under specific conditions: it emerges around a third of the way into training (not from initialization), differentiates around a specific middle layer in a roughly 40-layer model (DINOv2-g), and requires sufficient model scale — it is not observed below ViT-Large. It appears consistently across three different training paradigms tested (supervised, text-supervised via CLIP-style training, and self-supervised via DINOv2), which is itself an important piece of evidence discussed further below.

### 2.2 Direct evidence for the trade: local information lost, global information gained

A pair of probing results makes the trade-off concrete and quantitative, not merely qualitative. Probing outlier tokens for their own patch's spatial position gives markedly worse localization than probing an ordinary token (top-1 position accuracy of 22.8 for outliers versus 41.7 for normal tokens; average positional error 5.09 versus 0.79), and probing for pixel-level reconstruction of the patch's own content likewise shows substantially higher reconstruction error for outliers (25.23 versus 18.38) — direct evidence the outlier token has genuinely lost track of its own local patch's identity, not merely that it carries additional information *on top of* preserving that identity. At the same time, a single outlier token, used entirely on its own, classifies the whole image with accuracy that is far higher than an ordinary single token on most datasets, approaching the accuracy of the dedicated `[CLS]` classification token specifically designed to aggregate global image information — direct evidence the outlier token has gained access to genuinely global, image-level content, not merely a locally different representation of its own patch.

### 2.3 The intervention: explicit registers absorb the entire phenomenon

The paper's strongest evidence is a direct architectural intervention: add a small number of additional, **learnable register tokens** to the input sequence that carry no input information of their own (they are not tied to any specific image patch) and whose output is never used for any task loss (they are simply discarded at the end). A single added register token is already enough to make the visible patch-level outlier artifacts disappear entirely; four registers is used as the default in most of the paper's experiments, but the ablation shows one is sufficient to remove the artifact from ordinary patches. With registers present, the high-norm behavior does not vanish from the model — it moves entirely, and completely, into the registers themselves: patch-token norms show no outliers whatsoever, and a global-information probe run on the registers reaches accuracy (71.1 on the Aircraft dataset) close to what the original, patch-borne outlier tokens reached (73.3) before registers were added.

**This is a genuinely strong causal demonstration, worth being precise about why**: registers carry no information and receive no direct training signal from the task loss, yet the model chooses to route the same functional behavior into them the instant they are available — a direct demonstration that the underlying computational need (an internal scratchpad for global aggregation) is real and independent of any specific patch's content, and that its landing on ordinary background patches in the register-free baseline is simply what happens by default when no dedicated space is offered.

### 2.4 Robustness across training paradigms, and one informative non-effect

The register intervention removes the norm-outlier phenomenon consistently across DeiT-III (supervised), OpenCLIP (text-supervised), and DINOv2 (self-supervised) training — three genuinely different objectives, all producing the same underlying repurposing behavior and all responding the same way to the same fix. Downstream task performance mostly improves with registers added (dense prediction tasks and an unsupervised object-discovery method called LOST both improve substantially for DINOv2), **but not uniformly**: OpenCLIP's LOST score actually decreases slightly with registers added, a direct, honestly reported piece of evidence that removing the visible artifact does not automatically guarantee every downstream metric improves — "cleaner internal representations" and "better performance on every task" are not the same claim, and the paper does not conflate them.

One further honest limitation: masked autoencoders (MAE) do not show this outlier phenomenon at all, which the authors speculate is because MAE's patch-local reconstruction loss does not require the kind of global information aggregation that seems to drive the behavior elsewhere. This is offered as an observational comparison, not a controlled ablation, and the authors note MAE's frozen-representation downstream performance is generally lower than the other methods tested — so the absence of the artifact in MAE cannot cleanly be read as "MAE avoids a problem the others have," since MAE may simply not be doing the same kind of representation learning that creates the need for the scratchpad in the first place. The exact training conditions that determine which models develop the artifact are explicitly stated by the authors as not fully understood; the outlier-detection threshold used throughout (150) is manually chosen and varies somewhat across models, and the tendency toward low-information backgrounds is described as a statistical tendency, confounded to some degree by the object-centric nature of the training data and by artifacts introduced during positional-embedding interpolation.

### 2.5 What this establishes about autoregression and causality

**This is the critical non-autoregressive counterpoint the "Catch, Tag, Release" paper could not, on its own, provide.** Vision transformers use fully bidirectional self-attention with no causal mask and no sequential decoding of any kind, and yet the same underlying phenomenon — a structurally forced or opportunistically claimed slot repurposed as an internal, non-content-bearing computational resource — appears across three different training objectives. This directly rules out the strongest version of the claim that internal-register-style behavior requires the specific structural privilege a causal mask gives to a first position (continuous visibility, guaranteed self-attention). The paper's own framing is careful here: it states that the underlying mechanism "already appears naturally in Vision Transformers" and that adding explicit registers does not *create* this behavior but rather *isolates* an already-existing tendency, redirecting it away from patches that would otherwise be damaged by hosting it. **What this does not establish** is that every instance of an attention sink, in every architecture, shares an identical underlying mechanism with this ViT phenomenon — the paper measures a distinct object (norm-outlier patch tokens in vision transformers), not the same operational quantity measured in the language-model sink literature, and the two should not be silently equated. What it does establish, cleanly, is that the general *need* for an internal computational scratchpad, and the willingness to sacrifice whichever input slot is cheapest to sacrifice in order to get one, is not itself a consequence of autoregressive decoding or causal masking.

## 3. Reading the two papers together, and against the sink literature

The two papers answer complementary halves of one underlying question, and it is worth stating precisely which half each one answers, since it is tempting to read them as making the same claim when they do not.

**"Catch, Tag, Release" establishes what gets carried once a slot is claimed**: not nothing, but a shared, linearly decodable semantic direction, copied via the value vector into every token that attends there, later usable by downstream layers for selection or aggregation — evidenced through probing, PCA variance decomposition, and a sufficiency-only toy construction, entirely within causal, autoregressive language models.

**"Vision Transformers Need Registers" establishes that the underlying need for such a slot does not depend on autoregression, causal masking, or language at all**: the identical repurposing behavior (sacrifice a token's own local identity in exchange for a shared computational resource) appears under fully bidirectional attention, across three unrelated training objectives, and is cleanly, causally isolated by an intervention (adding content-free, output-free register tokens) that absorbs the entire phenomenon on contact.

**Read against the four attention-sink papers surveyed separately**: those four papers, taken together, establish that a sink's *formation* is driven overwhelmingly by structural properties (position-zero's forced self-attention under a causal mask, pre-norm architecture, head dimension, training context length) rather than by anything resembling semantic content or predictive uncertainty, and that the specific *fixed-anchor* form of the phenomenon does not survive the removal of the causal mask (the diffusion-transformer result). These two papers add the two pieces that account leaves open: first, that once a slot is claimed — for whatever structural reason — real, decodable, task-relevant content can and does get written into it via a mechanism (value-vector copying) that is separable from the attention pattern that created the slot in the first place; and second, that the underlying *need* for some kind of claimable internal-computation slot is itself independent of the causal, autoregressive machinery that the sink-formation literature focuses on, since bidirectional vision transformers develop and exploit an essentially identical opportunity with no causal structure available to explain it.

**For the token theory these notes support**, the operative distinction from [The Conversion Signature](theory-signature.md), section 2.5, is precisely the one both papers independently reinforce from different directions: **the mechanism that causes a slot to be selected or to form, and the content that slot ends up carrying once formed, are two separate questions, and conflating them is a type error, not a subtlety.** The sink-formation papers answer the first question (structural, largely content-independent). These two papers answer the second (real, readable, task-relevant content, once a slot exists) and, in the vision-transformer case, additionally show that the first question's answer generalizes well beyond the specific structural mechanism (a causal mask) that motivated asking it in language models in the first place.
