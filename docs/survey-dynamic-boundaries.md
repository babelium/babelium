# Dynamic Computation Boundaries: BLT and H-Net

This note covers two papers that replace fixed tokenization with a mechanism that decides, per position, whether the expensive part of a model should be invoked: the Byte Latent Transformer (Pagnoni et al., arXiv:2412.09871), which derives boundaries from a separately trained model's predictive entropy, and H-Net (Hwang et al., arXiv:2507.07955), which learns boundaries end-to-end from a similarity-based routing signal. Both are read here as evidence bearing on one question central to the token theory of [The Antinomy of the Token](token-antinomy.md) and its companion notes: what actually determines where a commitment boundary forms, and how much of that determination is genuinely about semantics versus something more purely structural or statistical. Both papers are strong, careful engineering work; the point of reading them this closely is to be precise about exactly what they do and do not establish about that question, since it is easy to read "learned boundaries that look linguistically sensible" as stronger evidence for a semantic account than the actual experiments support.

## 1. BLT: boundaries from predictive entropy

### 1.1 The mechanism

BLT replaces a fixed vocabulary with dynamically sized byte patches. A separate, smaller causal byte-level language model is trained on the same data distribution and estimates the entropy of the next byte at every position from the prefix alone. A patch boundary is placed either where this entropy exceeds a global threshold, or where it rises sufficiently relative to the immediately preceding position (a relative-rise rule, interpretable as breaking an otherwise roughly monotonically decreasing entropy trend within a patch). The large, expensive latent transformer runs once per patch rather than once per byte; a lightweight causal byte encoder and decoder handle byte-resolution processing on either side, with hashed n-gram embeddings and cross-attention needed, per the paper's own ablations, to close the gap with BPE-based baselines at larger patch sizes.

The central allocation logic, stated by the authors themselves: a large transformer is not needed to predict the ending of most words, since these are comparatively easy, low-entropy decisions relative to choosing the first word of a new sentence. Expensive computation is concentrated near locally hard choices; predictable continuations are grouped into longer patches that skip the expensive step.

### 1.2 What the evidence actually supports

The main results are compute-controlled comparisons, not a direct causal study of what a boundary "means." At compute-optimal training budgets, BLT matches or exceeds Llama-3-tokenized baselines up to 8B parameters; at fixed inference FLOPs, larger average patch sizes permit a larger latent model, eventually crossing over BPE-based models given sufficient training data. Entropy-based patching beats static, fixed-stride patching and is only modestly better than the much simpler heuristic of placing a boundary at every whitespace character. Scaling the auxiliary entropy model's capacity and context window improves the main model's loss, with diminishing returns past roughly 50M parameters and a 512-byte context. Byte-level access also yields sizable gains on noisy text, character-level manipulation tasks, and several low-resource translation directions, where a fixed subword vocabulary is a poor fit.

**Three qualifications, from the paper's own text, matter for how strongly this should be read as evidence for a semantic or cognitive boundary mechanism.** First, boundaries are produced offline by an auxiliary model trained *separately* from BLT — this is a data-driven heuristic, not an end-to-end-learned component of the main model, and the paper's own future-work section explicitly flags "learning the patching model in an end-to-end fashion" as an open direction it did not pursue. Second, repetition lowers entropy and produces "entropy drift": on structured, repetitive content such as multiple-choice question formats, entropy patching produces progressively larger patches precisely where a repeated reasoning template might still deserve global computation, and the authors' final large-scale run has to reset the entropy context at newlines and change the boundary rule to compensate — a sign that the mechanism's relationship to "where computation is actually needed" is imperfect even by the authors' own diagnosis. Third, the main downstream comparison at 8B parameters lowers the entropy threshold at inference time from 0.6 to 0.1, spending more latent-transformer steps in exchange for better task performance — a post-hoc, task-tuned adjustment of the very quantity the paper's headline scaling claims depend on, and one that is not present in the training-time boundary rule.

**The paper, read carefully, supports "predictive uncertainty is a useful proxy for where to allocate compute," not the stronger claim that semantic or cognitive commitment boundaries have been isolated.** Semantic structure enters only indirectly, through whatever contextual regularities the auxiliary byte-level LM happens to use in predicting the next byte; there is no semantic-boundary supervision, no semantic-boundary label, and no quantitative measurement of semantic-boundary accuracy anywhere in the paper. Space patching — placing a boundary at every whitespace character, a heuristic with no learned or entropy-based component at all — remains a close competitor throughout.

## 2. H-Net: boundaries learned end-to-end

### 2.1 The mechanism

H-Net replaces both fixed tokenization and BLT's external entropy heuristic with a chunking module trained jointly with the rest of the model, and structured recursively: each stage has an encoder at the current resolution, a learned routing layer that selects which positions to retain, a larger inner network operating on the shortened sequence, and a matching dechunking/decoder path with a residual connection back to the encoder. The outer, high-resolution encoder and decoder use Mamba-2 (a state-space layer, chosen because it empirically compresses context better at that resolution); the innermost network, operating on the much shorter chunked sequence, can be an ordinary transformer.

The boundary signal is not entropy. Given causal encoder hidden states, the router projects the current state into a query vector and the previous state into a key vector, computes a boundary score as one minus the cosine similarity between the two, divided by two, and hard-thresholds that score at 0.5. The motivating assumption, stated directly by the authors: at points of contextual or semantic shift, adjacent representations should become less similar, and this dissimilarity is what the router is designed to detect. Because both projections are themselves learned parameters, this score is a **learned geometric quantity**, not a calibrated probability and not a predictive-entropy estimate in BLT's sense — a distinction the paper itself is careful to state (it calls the score a boundary "confidence," notes it is "sometimes" referred to as a probability, and explicitly says it is not used as a formal probability). A separate ratio loss supplies pressure toward a target average compression rate (the total budget), since the byte-level prediction loss alone supplies no such pressure; gradients reach the discrete, hard-thresholded selection decision through a combination of confidence-weighted exponential smoothing during upsampling and a straight-through estimator.

At inference, the router makes its decision online from only the current and previous causal states — genuinely end-to-end conditional computation with no separately trained auxiliary model or preprocessing step. This is the central methodological difference from BLT. But the *total* compression rate is still externally chosen through the ratio loss's target value, so H-Net learns *where within a largely predetermined budget* to spend expensive computation, not what the total budget itself should be.

### 2.2 What the evidence actually supports

The evidence is broad and mostly compute- or data-matched. A single-stage H-Net beats both fixed pooling and a strong whitespace-boundary baseline; a two-stage (recursively nested) H-Net further improves both loss and downstream accuracy over the single-stage version. On English text, the first stage's learned boundaries tend toward spaces and word starts, while a second stage sometimes groups whole words into phrase-like units. On text with whitespace deliberately removed, the learned boundaries can still be preserved, suggesting the router is not merely keying on the whitespace character itself. H-Net also outperforms tokenizer-and-space-based baselines on Chinese and on code, two domains where naive whitespace segmentation is a poor heuristic, and improves data efficiency on DNA sequences, a domain with no natural "words" at all. Component ablations show the confidence-smoothing mechanism is essential for stable compression-rate training (removing it causes the realized compression ratio to fluctuate severely and prevents the model from learning consistent boundaries at all); replacing the cosine-similarity router with a simpler direct predictor, and removing the straight-through estimator, both cause smaller but measurable degradations.

**The main semantic evidence remains qualitative.** The paper's central visualization (Figure 4 in the original) offers selected examples of learned chunks such as "the backbone" or "such as," but there is no annotated boundary benchmark, no calibrated-uncertainty comparison against BLT-style entropy, and no counterfactual intervention that moves semantic content while holding surface statistics fixed (or vice versa) to isolate what the router is actually responding to. The stronger quantitative comparisons unavoidably conflate the effect of learned routing itself with a number of other H-Net-specific architectural choices — normalization, residual structure, learning-rate schedule, the choice of Mamba-2 for the outer layers, width, depth, and how parameters are allocated across stages — and while staged baseline comparisons reduce this confound, they do not eliminate it. BLT itself is never run as a direct baseline in the same paper, so the two mechanisms are not compared head-to-head under matched conditions. The largest reported experiments are equivalent to only a 1.3B-parameter transformer; no formal scaling law is fitted (the authors state this is due to resource constraints, not a finding that scaling behaves poorly); reported tokenizer-based bits-per-byte comparisons are explicitly acknowledged by the authors to be inexact; and the dynamic, conditional-computation implementation can train up to roughly twice as slowly in wall-clock time as an equivalent fixed-computation model, a cost not reflected in FLOP-based comparisons.

## 3. Reading the two papers together

BLT and H-Net answer the same underlying design question — where should an expensive computation step be invoked — with genuinely different mechanisms, and it is worth being precise about exactly how they differ, since casual summary tends to collapse the distinction.

**BLT's signal is calibrated predictive uncertainty from a separately trained model**: the next-byte entropy of an auxiliary causal language model, thresholded either absolutely or relative to the preceding position. Its boundary decision is not trained jointly with the main model at all; it is a fixed, externally computed heuristic that happens to be data-driven rather than rule-based (unlike a whitespace heuristic), but is not learned end-to-end with the objective the main model is trained on.

**H-Net's signal is a learned geometric dissimilarity between adjacent hidden-state projections**, trained jointly and end-to-end with the main byte-prediction objective via a straight-through estimator, with a separate loss term controlling only the aggregate rate of boundary placement, not their locations. Locations are learned; the total budget is externally targeted.

**Neither paper's boundary mechanism has been shown, by direct measurement, to track semantic content specifically, as opposed to predictability (BLT) or representational dissimilarity (H-Net) that merely correlates with semantic content in the domains tested.** Both papers report evidence consistent with a semantic interpretation (BLT's low-entropy word endings, H-Net's phrase-like groupings) but neither isolates semantics from the more basic quantity its own mechanism actually measures. This matters directly for the theory this note supports: within the vocabulary of that theory, both mechanisms are best read as **evidence about where sel is placed and what quantity governs its placement**, not as evidence that the placement is driven by anything one would want to call "meaning" per se. BLT's mechanism is closest to entropy as an operational proxy for commitment cost, exactly the proxy flagged (and cautioned against over-interpreting) in the companion theory notes; H-Net's mechanism is closer to a learned, end-to-end version of the same underlying question, with the crucial difference that the *quantity* triggering commitment is now itself learned rather than derived from an independently estimated distribution, at the cost of losing the interpretability that comes with a well-defined entropy estimate.

**A natural, still-open experiment follows directly from placing the two side by side**, and both papers' own limitations sections point toward it independently: run BLT's entropy-based router and H-Net's learned cosine router as two arms under matched training data, matched compute, and a matched target compression rate, and measure not only downstream performance but the two routers' agreement with each other's boundary placements and with any available annotated linguistic-unit boundaries. Neither paper does this comparison, and the strongest available evidence at present is comparing each paper's numbers against its *own* internal baselines (space patching for BLT, fixed pooling for H-Net) rather than against the other paper's mechanism directly.
