# Attention Sinks: Four Papers on Where They Come From, What They Do, and Where They Stop Applying

This note covers four papers examining the attention-sink phenomenon (the tendency of attention to concentrate disproportionate mass on a small number of positions, most famously the first token) from four different angles: why sinks help at all (Barbero et al., "Why do LLMs attend to the first token?", arXiv:2504.02732), the specific two-block mechanism that produces a sink at position zero (arXiv:2603.06591), the relationship between sinks and a separate phenomenon called massive activations (Sun, Canziani, LeCun, and Zhu, arXiv:2603.05498), and what happens to sink-like concentration once the causal, autoregressive structure that seems to explain sinks in language models is removed entirely (a causal analysis of sinks in diffusion transformers, arXiv:2605.09313). Together they give a layered account: a structural explanation for why sinks form at all, a mechanistic circuit for how one specific instance forms, a demonstration that sinks and a superficially related phenomenon are actually dissociable, and a genuine cross-architecture test of how much of the story survives outside autoregressive decoding. This note was written as literature background for the commitment-and-abstention discussion in the token theory's companion notes ([The Conversion Signature](theory-signature.md), section on absence, and [The Conversion Signature](theory-signature.md)'s discussion of commitment); it is read here for what it actually establishes, not for what a looser summary might suggest it establishes.

## 1. Why do LLMs attend to the first token? (Barbero et al., arXiv:2504.02732)

### 1.1 The claim and its mechanism

This paper argues, theoretically and empirically, that the first-token sink gives transformers a way to avoid **over-mixing**: attention, applied repeatedly across layers, acts as a mixing operator, and as depth and context length grow, a perturbation to one token's representation propagates further into the representations of every other token, pushing toward representational collapse. Diverting a portion of attention mass toward a low-norm beginning-of-sequence (BOS) value lets certain attention heads default to an approximate no-op — updating their output very little — reducing the effective rate of mixing across the sequence; a head can then switch to attending to genuinely informative content only when its specific triggering condition is met. A theoretical Jacobian bound connects depth, head count, and context length to sensitivity to perturbation, and the paper's experiments are designed to test the predictions this bound makes.

### 1.2 What the experiments show, and their limits

On Gemma 7B, replacing a word with a same-length synonym and comparing the presence versus absence of BOS shows a perturbation propagating more strongly across layers and positions once BOS is removed, and several attention heads become measurably smoother (less peaked) without it. One specific head is shown to behave as an approximate conditional no-op: it attends to an apostrophe when a specific condition is met, and to BOS otherwise — consistent with BOS serving as a low-cost default target precisely because its value has the smallest norm among candidates. Training small (roughly 120M-parameter) models from scratch while varying only context length, holding total training tokens fixed at 5B across models, shows sink strength increasing systematically with context length (with essentially no sink at a length of 128), while validation loss curves stay comparable across the different lengths — a genuine controlled manipulation rather than an observational correlation. Across the Llama 3.1 family (8B, 70B, 405B), a sink-strength metric increases with model scale (45.97, 73.49, 78.29 respectively at one fixed threshold) — though this comparison is within one model family and does not control training data or training budget, so it is evidence of a within-family correlation, not an independent causal manipulation of scale.

A direct intervention removes the sink by removing BOS: models trained with a fixed BOS position show sink strength collapsing to near zero at inference when BOS is removed, and loss rising sharply (from roughly 2.69 to between 7.56 and 7.78); downstream tasks on Gemma 7B degrade substantially with BOS removed, most dramatically on a long-context benchmark (RULER-4096 falling from 82.57 to 0). **But removing a token that was always present during training simultaneously shifts the input distribution out of training-time conditions**, so the resulting performance drop cannot be attributed purely to loss of the sink mechanism as opposed to simple distribution shift — the paper's own framing narrows the causal claim to "the model depends on having a stable first-position slot," which is a real finding, but weaker than "removing the sink specifically causes the loss."

Notably, when training without any fixed BOS token at all, a sink still forms at whatever token happens to occupy the first position, only slightly weaker than with a dedicated BOS — a direct piece of evidence that **the sink's function does not depend on the specific semantic identity of the token occupying position zero**, only on that position's structural property of being both earliest and always visible under a causal mask. The paper's scope is explicitly restricted to causal decoder-only language models; there is no encoder, no vision transformer, no diffusion model tested, and no direct measurement of predictive entropy or semantic boundaries as an alternative explanatory variable.

## 2. A mechanistic circuit for the P0 sink (arXiv:2603.06591)

### 2.1 The two-block circuit

This paper proposes a specific, two-block mechanism — the **P0-Sink Circuit** — for how a sink at position zero forms within a shallow number of layers, independent of any particular BOS token's learned embedding. The first block, **position-zero identification**, is a direct arithmetic consequence of the causal mask: position zero's attention weight on itself is forced to exactly 1 (it has nothing else to attend to), so its attention output is never mixed with any other position's content and is consequently more stable in direction, and has a higher expected squared norm, than the output of any other position (whose attention weight necessarily gets spread across more than one key, each contributing less than a full unit of weight). The second block, **fixed-direction amplification**, has the subsequent MLP sublayer detect this norm asymmetry and push position zero's representation toward a consistent, high-norm, fixed direction across different inputs — in a pre-norm architecture, what downstream layers actually see is direction rather than raw norm, and a higher-norm residual makes that direction more stable against training-time perturbations (formalized via a local linearization of the normalization operation).

Evidence for this circuit comes from LLaMA 3.1-8B: layer-wise norm tracking, cross-sample cosine similarity, and PCA/t-SNE visualization show that even with BOS removed, position zero separates from other positions and becomes a high-norm, fixed-direction representation within the first two blocks. A precise subtraction of one specific layer-0 attention head's contribution sharpens the visualized clustering, though this head-removal step is described as a visualization aid to reduce confounding, not a complete circuit-level knockout experiment establishing necessity.

### 2.2 The critical boundary case: architecture-dependence

Removing BOS from LLaMA models causes the sink rate to drop in only the first two layers, recovering to near its original level from layer two onward — support for a positional mechanism independent of the BOS embedding specifically. **Mistral is a genuine counterexample**: removing BOS collapses its sink rate from roughly 87% to roughly 2%. The paper's explanation is architectural: Mistral's sliding-window attention means position zero is not continuously visible to all later positions once the window slides past it, so the model never develops the same kind of stable, purely positional P0 sink and instead relies entirely on the BOS token's specific learned embedding. In hybrid architectures mixing sliding-window and full-attention layers, sliding-window layers show no P0 sink while full-attention layers in the same model do — a clean within-model comparison that further pins the mechanism's applicability to continuous visibility under a causal mask, not to autoregressive decoding or transformers in general.

A repeated-first-token experiment finds that repeating a non-BOS content token produces a smaller loss increase in LLaMA than repeating BOS itself, which the authors interpret as evidence that the structural P0 position is a more stable "semantic vacuum" than a content-bearing token would be — but this is an indirect, functional measure (loss under a perturbation), not a direct demonstration (via probing or content-decoding) that the P0 representation itself carries no decodable semantic content at all.

### 2.3 Two engineering interventions, and their mixed results

The paper introduces two parameter-free training modifications motivated by this circuit. **TNLM loss** removes the cross-entropy term for predicting the first real token from the BOS position, on the reasoning that this prediction objective competes with, and interferes with, the gradient that would otherwise let BOS specialize purely as a sink register. **Force-sink masking** hard-partitions the residual stream at every layer via a fixed binary mask, confining position zero's representation to a fixed k-dimensional subspace and every other token to the complementary subspace — directly implementing both position identification and cross-position separation by construction, rather than relying on the model to discover them.

Training roughly 1B-parameter models on 20B tokens (a single seed, three peak learning rates) shows both interventions beating their respective baselines on average downstream score, with Force-sink often matching or slightly exceeding a separate Gated Attention baseline. Force-sink substantially raises sink rate while simultaneously *lowering* the magnitude of massive activation outliers at position zero — direct evidence that a large activation outlier is not a necessary ingredient for a sink to form, only one way it happens to manifest under the default recipe. At a larger, more data-saturated 100B-token scale, the picture is considerably messier: the untrained baseline already has a high sink rate, the Gated Attention comparison's average advantage disappears, TNLM's gain shrinks to marginal, and different Force-sink variants are not uniformly better than baseline at every checkpoint. **The paper's own most defensible summary is that accelerating early sink formation co-occurs with better results at the 20B scale, not that a higher sink rate is unconditionally better** — a distinction the paper itself is careful to preserve rather than overstate. The value of k in Force-sink masking is manually recalibrated between the two training scales (16 at 20B tokens, 32 at 100B), with the authors explicitly acknowledging no principled criterion exists yet for choosing it.

### 2.4 What is, and is not, established

The strongest, most reliably supported claim is the structural one: a fixed P0 boundary can be initiated purely by the positional asymmetry the causal mask itself creates, requiring no specific BOS semantics, and this reconstitutes within a shallow number of layers even without a dedicated BOS token — subsequently consolidated by the MLP into a high-norm, fixed-direction register. The weaker, less directly supported claim is that accelerating this formation causes better downstream performance; the paper's own single-seed comparisons, its scale-dependent inconsistency, and the confound of the interventions simultaneously changing the training objective (TNLM) or the representational subspace and optimization geometry (Force-sink) mean the causal chain from "faster sink formation" to "better performance" has not been isolated from these co-occurring changes via mediation analysis or a sink-rate-matched control.

## 3. Massive activations and sinks: related but dissociable (Sun, Canziani, LeCun, Zhu, arXiv:2603.05498)

### 3.1 The relationship, and where it comes apart

This paper studies a phenomenon that frequently co-occurs with attention sinks — **massive activations**, a small number of channels reaching extreme magnitude in intermediate layers — and shows the two are related but not the same thing, nor necessarily bound together. Its central identification: the **pre-norm** configuration is the specific architectural choice that enables their co-occurrence in standard practice, and ablating pre-norm causes the two phenomena to decouple. Mechanistically, early blocks amplify a small number of channels; the pre-norm residual stream lets these extreme values accumulate across the middle of the network; RMSNorm compresses different "spike" tokens into a sparse, nearly constant direction, so that a spike token's key vector becomes a low-dimensional, cross-prompt-stable reference point; and only those attention heads whose query subspace happens to align with that particular key subspace end up forming a sink around it. Which token becomes the initial spike is shown to be almost entirely a **positional**, not semantic, effect: cycling every vocabulary token through position zero across seven different models makes 98.40% to 99.93% of them become the spike token — near-universal, and clearly not driven by any specific token's meaning.

### 3.2 What the ablations actually separate

A series of from-scratch training ablations on a roughly Llama-style 7B model (100B DCLM tokens) systematically decouples the two phenomena. Replacing standard pre-norm with Sandwich(QK) normalization crushes the spike magnitude (from 3818 down to 92) while the sink ratio barely moves (46.0% down to 42.0%) — spikes can be removed while sinks persist. DynamicTanh normalization does the opposite in a sense: spike magnitude falls to 153, but sink ratio actually *rises* to 61.0%, with no measurable cost to perplexity — a second, independent demonstration that spike magnitude and sink strength are not the same quantity moving together. Varying head dimension while holding total model capacity fixed shows sink ratio rising monotonically from 4.1% to 46.0% as head dimension grows from 8 to 128 — fewer, wider heads form sinks more readily, a purely architectural lever with no obvious semantic interpretation. Giving heads an explicit, representation-conditioned gate (letting the model decide per-channel or per-head how much to attend, based on current content rather than position) drops sink ratio to 4.5% or 6.4%, while a static positional or token-identity-based gate leaves it largely unchanged at 41.1% or 31.1% — evidence that an explicit, content-conditioned routing mechanism can substitute for the sink's routing function, while a static one cannot. And restricting the training loss to only compute over long positions (2048 to 4096), rather than including short positions, collapses sink ratio to 1.2% with comparable perplexity — directly implicating the training-time context-length distribution itself as a driver of sink formation, not merely a correlate of it.

### 3.3 The resulting division of roles

The paper's synthesis: massive activations function as **global implicit parameters** — a small set of channels the whole model can lean on regardless of input — while attention sinks function as **local, head-specific, input-conditioned modulators**, giving individual heads a "dumping ground" that lets them effectively ignore long-range context when that context is not predictive. Their co-occurrence in standard pretrained models "is best understood as a byproduct of the default normalization and training recipe, not a reflection of any underlying functional necessity" — a direct statement, in the paper's own words, against reading the co-occurrence as evidence that the two are mechanistically the same thing.

All of this evidence comes from decoder-only, pre-norm, causally-masked training on next-token prediction — the paper does not test non-causal, encoder, vision-transformer, or diffusion architectures, and its own summary is explicit that it studies "two phenomena that reliably co-occur in decoder-only, pre-norm Transformers," not a universal claim about all transformer variants.

## 4. Attention sinks in diffusion transformers: what survives without autoregression (arXiv:2605.09313)

### 4.1 A different object: dynamically defined sinks, not a fixed P0 anchor

This paper asks a pointed question: does anything like an attention sink appear in a **diffusion** transformer, which has no causal mask, no autoregressive decoding, and no fixed first position — and if something does appear, does it play the same functional role a language-model sink plays? Sinks here are defined **dynamically** and **operationally**: for each key position j, average the attention weight every query assigns to it, and call the top-k positions by this average, computed separately per attention head, per layer, and per denoising timestep, the sinks at that point. On Stable Diffusion 3, the strongest concentration appears in a middle layer (layer 12), with the single top position receiving roughly 9.5% of incoming mass and low attention entropy (roughly 4.0 to 4.5), strongest during the early, high-noise phase of denoising and weakening as denoising proceeds. **These dynamically identified sinks essentially never coincide with index 0** (an overlap rate below 0.2%, usually below 0.1%), and instead cluster within a narrow band of the joint sequence (roughly positions 4016 to 4231) that is almost entirely text-conditioning keys (47,999 out of 48,000 sampled instances) — so "dynamic" here means a localized drift within a small subset of text-conditioning positions, not movement across the full space of possible keys, and the paper does not further decompose which specific text positions (padding, structural tokens, content-bearing tokens) these are.

### 4.2 Causal intervention: necessity for alignment, versus effect on the trajectory

The paper runs seed-and-prompt-matched, training-free interventions on 553 GenEval prompts with Stable Diffusion 3, along two paths: a **score-path** intervention that hard-masks the pre-softmax logits of the identified top-k sink positions before softmax renormalization (fully suppressing their contribution), and a **value-path** intervention that replaces the sink's own value vector with zero, the mean value, or an interpolation toward the mean, testing whether the specific content held at that position matters.

The standard, minimal intervention (masking only the single top position, k=1, at layer 12) reduces incoming mass at that position from roughly 10% to under 0.001% — a reduction of more than eight orders of magnitude, confirming the intervention genuinely removes the targeted contribution — while CLIP-T (the primary semantic-alignment proxy metric) changes by only -0.0005, with a confidence interval straddling zero; masking layers 6, 12, and 18 simultaneously shows no clear degradation either. **Yet the same intervention produces a substantial, measurably specific change to the generated image's trajectory**: perceptual distance (LPIPS) from the unmodified baseline reaches roughly 0.19 to 0.31 depending on intervention strength, and to isolate whether this is simply an artifact of removing *any* token's contribution, the authors compare against an equal-budget *random* key-masking control — sink masking at k=1 produces LPIPS of roughly 0.347 versus 0.053 for random masking of the same budget, a gap of about six times, with the gap widening further at k=5. **This dissociation is the paper's central finding**: high incoming attention mass at the identified positions is not necessary for the coarse semantic alignment that CLIP-T and similar proxies measure, while it does carry substantial, sink-specific information about the concrete visual realization — layout, color, viewpoint, and style — of the generated image. The sink is not "doing nothing"; it is doing something the tested semantic-alignment proxies simply do not register as loss.

### 4.3 The boundary of "safe to remove"

Scanning a range of intervention strengths (k = 1, 5, 10, 20, 50) at layer 12 shows CLIP-T's confidence interval straddling zero across the whole range — the semantic-alignment proxy stays robust even under fairly aggressive suppression. But a *preference*-oriented proxy, HPS-v2, behaves differently: the sink-versus-random difference-of-differences is not significant at k=1, but becomes -0.005 at k=10 and -0.020 at k=50 — a dose-dependent, sink-specific degradation in preference score that only appears under stronger suppression. **So "sinks can be safely removed" holds specifically for standard, small-budget interventions and the specific proxy metrics tested here; it does not generalize to arbitrarily strong suppression, and it does not generalize to every metric one might care about.** This boundary is exactly the kind of qualification easy to drop in an informal retelling of the result, and the paper is careful to state it explicitly in its own conclusion.

### 4.4 What this establishes about the causal-versus-diffusion contrast

The result the paper's own framing supports most directly is this: attention concentration (something that looks, superficially, like an attention sink) still forms under non-causal, bidirectional, iterative-refinement diffusion inference, ruling out the possibility that sink-like behavior is *inherently* tied to autoregressive, causally-masked decoding. But what forms is **not a fixed, load-bearing P0 anchor of the kind found in causal language models** — it is a dynamic, phase-dependent, layer-and-timestep-varying routing point that the tested semantic-alignment proxies do not depend on, even though the underlying generative trajectory clearly does. The paper's own stated caution deserves to be quoted directly, since it is easy to over-read this result in either direction: "these findings caution against transferring autoregressive sink intuitions to diffusion transformers, and suggest that high incoming attention mass need not indicate functional necessity for alignment in non-autoregressive generation." This is evidence that a form of attention concentration does not strictly require autoregression to appear, and evidence that its *functional role* changes substantially once autoregression is removed — not evidence that "sinks are unnecessary in general," and not evidence that any strength of removal is safe.

## 5. Reading the four papers together

The four papers, read as a set, support a layered account rather than a single unified claim, and it matters to keep the layers distinct.

**Why sinks are useful at all** (Barbero et al.): concentrating attention mass onto a stable, low-norm position lets certain heads approximate a no-op, slowing the rate at which a perturbation to one token's representation propagates to every other token as depth and context length grow — a genuine functional account of *why* such a mechanism would be selected for, tested via controlled context-length ablations and cross-scale comparison.

**How one specific instance mechanically forms** (arXiv:2603.06591): under a causal mask, position zero's attention weight is forced to exactly one by simple arithmetic, giving it an unmixed, high-norm, direction-stable attention output that the subsequent MLP amplifies into a fixed-direction register — a mechanistic circuit for the causal-LLM P0 case specifically, explicitly shown *not* to generalize to sliding-window architectures like Mistral, where the mechanism instead depends on the specific BOS embedding because position zero is not continuously visible.

**What co-occurs with sinks but is not the same thing** (Sun, Canziani, LeCun, Zhu): massive activations and attention sinks typically appear together in standard pre-norm causal transformers, but a series of independent architectural and training-time ablations (normalization choice, head dimension, explicit gating, restricted context-length training) show the two phenomena can be decoupled from each other, and each can be independently manipulated or removed — the co-occurrence is a byproduct of a shared default recipe, not evidence of a single underlying mechanism producing both.

**What happens once autoregression is removed entirely** (the diffusion causal analysis): attention concentration still forms, but it is dynamic, dissociated from any fixed position, does not carry weight the tested semantic-alignment proxies need, yet still carries information the generative trajectory clearly uses — a genuine test of how much of the causal-LM story is about autoregression specifically versus about attention mechanisms more generally, with the answer coming back as "some of it transfers, and some of it does not."

**For the token theory these notes support**, the throughline worth keeping precise: none of the four papers establishes that a sink's formation is driven by anything resembling semantic content or predictive uncertainty in the sense the theory's own commitment framework uses those terms. The strongest, most carefully controlled evidence across all four papers points toward **structural** triggers — position-zero identification under a causal mask, architectural choices like pre-norm and head dimension, and training-time distributional properties like context length — with semantics entering, when it enters at all, only as a downstream consequence of a slot the structure has already carved out (the "semantic vacuum" framing in arXiv:2603.06591, and the "global implicit parameter" versus "local input-conditioned modulator" split in arXiv:2603.05498). The diffusion paper's contribution is to show precisely where this structural story stops holding without qualification: remove the causal mask and the fixed-anchor version of the phenomenon disappears, replaced by something dynamic whose relationship to any downstream-necessary function is considerably weaker and more metric-dependent than the causal-LM literature's framing might suggest to a reader who has only encountered the causal case.
