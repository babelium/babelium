# The Antinomy of the Token

## Prologue: A Conversation Cut Off Mid-Word

Suppose you are using a code completion tool. You are halfway through typing, and the cursor sits in the middle of a word — say you have just typed `the reader is intri`, without finishing `intrigued`, and the tool has to continue from there.

Normally, if you hand the complete sentence `the reader is intrigued` to a language model, the model first cuts it into a string of word fragments (the technical term is *token*; what that actually is will be made precise below). The word `intrigued` will most likely be cut into two pieces such as `intrig` + `ued`, or perhaps stay whole — which cut you get depends on how often the word appears in the training data and on the tokenizer's merge rules.

But right now you have typed only `intri`. The tool cannot foresee that you are about to type `gued`; it can only tokenize the five letters it has. And the cut that a tokenizer gives for `intri` alone is very likely **not the same cut** as the prefix of the cut it gives for the complete word. If the complete word becomes `intrig` + `ued`, then the bare `intri`, unable to assemble the fragment `intrig`, may come out as something entirely different, like `int` + `ri`.

What follows from that? During training, the language model has only ever seen token sequences produced by cutting complete words the way complete words get cut. It has never seen `int` + `ri` appear in the position occupied by `intrigued`. So when you feed it this unusual cut, it reads the current position as one where a word has just ended and a new one should begin — it will very likely insert a space and start a fresh word, rather than finishing `intrigued`.

This is not a hypothetical anecdote. It is a real engineering problem with a real name: **token healing**. The standard fix is to throw away the last one or two fragments before the cursor and regenerate that stretch under a more conservative, byte-constrained scheme, so the model can "heal" and pick up what should have been there.

The patch itself is not hard; it is routine engineering by now. But behind it sits a question that has never been asked all the way down. A tokenizer cuts text into a string of discrete pieces, and the model interprets those pieces back into text or continues from them — over that round trip, what guarantees that "cut apart and reassembled" and "left as it was" are the same thing? And further: if that guarantee can fail in the most ordinary situation imaginable — typing half a sentence — then what kind of object is a token, such that its behavior is this unstable?

What this article describes is the process of digging into that question. Dig far enough and you find that the claim almost everyone would unreflectively accept — *a token is a piece that text or a signal has been cut into* — describes only half of what a token is.

## Chapter 1: The Assumption Nobody Ever Stated

Nearly every discussion of tokens, whether about language model tokenizers or about how image models cut a picture into patches, carries the same subtext: **a token is a slice of the signal**. Text is cut into substrings, an image into a grid of pixel blocks, audio into frames. The claim is so natural that almost nobody treats it as a hypothesis in need of testing.

But move your gaze away from text tokenization and look at how other fields *manufacture* their own tokens, and the slicing story starts to run out.

In vector quantization (VQ, covered in detail in a later case study), the situation is nearly the reverse. The model first computes a continuous, high-dimensional representation, then finds the nearest entry in a codebook prepared in advance and takes that entry as the token for this piece of content. That is not "cutting the signal apart"; it is **snapping** the signal onto an anchor fixed beforehand. Slicing goes from whole to part; snapping goes from continuous to discrete. The direction and the mechanism differ.

So step back and split the word *token* into the two capacities it actually carries, rather than rushing to define it.

One capacity is **identity**: a token has to be countable, retrievable by index, computable once and storable for reuse, recognizable as "the same one" across different occasions, and composable like a building block into longer sequences. These requirements sound abstract but are concrete in practice — token number 5 in a vocabulary is still number 5 whether it appears at the start of one sentence or the middle of another. That is identity at work. It is exactly this property that lets a language model use a KV cache: compute the earlier content once and reuse it, instead of recomputing every time, because the address "number 5" is itself stable and does not vary with context.

The other capacity is **similarity**: being able to compare how alike two things are, to interpolate between two points, and, on encountering an unseen example, to infer how to handle it from what it resembles. This demands a continuous space that can be differentiated and moved around in — the high-dimensional space where embedding vectors live.

These two capacities correspond to two entirely different mathematical structures, discrete and continuous. And the sentence "a token is a slice of the signal" emphasizes only the identity half — every piece cut out can be counted and numbered. It says nothing about the similarity half: whether two tokens can be compared, interpolated, judged alike.

So picture a token as a two-faced object: one face turned toward *countable, addressable, cacheable, composable*, the other toward *comparable, interpolable, generalizable to unseen examples*. The two faces look unrelated — one is set-theoretic and discrete, the other geometric and continuous — yet every time a language model runs, both are in use at once. The input text is first cut into discrete tokens (identity face), each token is translated into a continuous embedding vector for computation (similarity face), and the result must be translated back into discrete tokens to become output text (identity face again).

The **conversion** between the two faces is the thing actually worth watching. And that conversion necessarily contains one decisive act — picking a discrete item out of the continuous, or placing a discrete item into the continuous. That act will be given a name of its own later: **commitment**. Remember the word; nearly every case study below turns around it.

## Chapter 2: Five Case Studies, Five Ways of Getting Back

The abstractions are in place; now for actual examples. The five cases below come from five entirely different model families, but all of them are doing the same thing: converting back and forth between the continuous and the discrete. Working through them shows how the same conversion grows completely different symptoms in different places.

### Case A: Autoregressive Text Generation — The Perpetually Misread First Word

Autoregressive language models (AR from here on — models that emit one token after another) are the example most often reached for in any discussion of tokens, and precisely because they are so familiar, some genuinely structural facts about them tend to get waved past.

Start with a phenomenon everyone has noticed but whose cause has never been stated clearly: **why does the first generated token always look slightly odd, almost like a placeholder?**

To answer that, a word about what a language model looks like inside. For every new token, the model runs the whole text written so far through an **attention** mechanism — roughly, it lets the current position look at every preceding position and decide which to weigh and which to ignore, the way a speaker glances over everyone else's notes before contributing. And because generation proceeds left to right, when writing token *t* the model may look only at positions before *t*, never ahead. That rule is the **causal mask**: causes may be seen, effects may not.

The causal mask has an immediate corollary: the first position in a sequence, whenever you look at it, can see nothing but itself. This is not a strategy the model learned; it is an arithmetic consequence of a hard rule. And because that position can only ever see itself, the attention weights it produces follow a very fixed pattern that does not vary with content, and the layers above shape it into a special **anchor**. Many papers call this an **attention sink**: other positions, when computing attention, pour inexplicably large amounts of weight into this first position, as if information were draining into a sink.

One popular explanation has been that this position performs *semantic* placeholding, or marks some boundary of high certainty where no work is needed. Following the thread down gives a more accurate account: **the position's specialness is, first of all, purely structural.** The causal mask alone, with no semantics involved, already locks the first position into an island that sees no one and only itself. That the model later learns to use it as a sink is a natural consequence of this structural property, not evidence that some semantic judgment came first and produced the specialness.

That conclusion is not itself new — the attention-sink literature has clean circuit-level evidence for it. But it raises a deeper question. Slots forced into existence by structure appear widely across models: the **register** slots in vision models discussed later, `[MASK]` in masked language models, `padding` at the end of a sequence. These slots share one feature: nothing in the original input signal corresponds to them. No character in a passage of text corresponds to `[MASK]`; no patch of pixels in an image corresponds to a `register`. Such slots are bolted on, not grown out of the signal. Case E returns to this.

So much for the first token. The more dramatic part of this case is a self-refutation.

An early stage of the theory judged AR to be a structural **outlier**. In text generation, the model first scores every candidate in the vocabulary (those scores, stacked together, live in a space whose dimension equals the vocabulary size — tens of thousands of dimensions if the vocabulary has tens of thousands of entries), then selects the highest-scoring token and converts it into a vector of a few hundred or few thousand dimensions (the *embedding space*, where the model's actual computation happens), which is passed to the next step. At first glance the scoring space (tens of thousands of dimensions) and the space fed forward (a few hundred) have different dimensions, so this conversion would seem to require a bridge between two entirely different spaces — whereas in vector quantization, scoring and feeding forward happen in the same space. Hence the early judgment: AR is the only placement that must cross between two different spaces; the others need no crossing.

That judgment turned out to be wrong, and the manner of the error is worth recounting, because the way it went wrong points at an easily missed trap.

The mistake was treating the **score table** as the space where the carrier lives. The model scores each candidate by taking the inner product of the current hidden state with a vector associated with each vocabulary entry — the inner product being the sum of elementwise products, larger when the two vectors point in more nearly the same direction. The current hidden state and the embedding vector of the selected token **have always lived in the same few-hundred-dimensional space**; they were never apart. The "tens of thousands of scores" are just a table of numbers produced by this inner product, not the place where the hidden state or the selected vector *resides* — much as ranking a room of people by height gives you a table with a hundred ranks, but each person's height is a single number, not an element of "rank space." The ranking table and the height are not the same kind of object.

Once that is clear, AR is doing the **same operation** as the vector quantization model below: take a continuous vector, compare it against a batch of anchor vectors, select the closest, hand that anchor onward. The only difference is the comparison — AR uses an inner product, VQ uses Euclidean distance — and those two agree on the ranking only when all anchor vectors have equal norm. That is the one residual difference, a purely numerical detail, not a gulf between spaces.

**The lesson left by this self-correction: AR went from "the one structural outlier in the whole theory" to an ordinary instance isomorphic to every other placement.** And the reason "AR is an outlier" seemed plausible at first is precisely that AR is the example nearly everyone meets first — an atypical case that became the default mental picture behind the word *token*. That may partly explain why discussion of tokenization and text generation has stayed so long on empirical analogies about *compression* and *boundaries*: everyone has been arguing over the interface hardest to compare directly, while the other interfaces, where the gap is far easier to measure, went unexamined.

Two more structural details about AR, both concerning the asymmetry between training and generation.

First: during **training**, the model never actually performs the act of "picking a token." Training uses *teacher forcing* — whatever the model would have predicted, the next step is fed the ground-truth token from the dataset. The act of selection happens exactly once, at **generation** time. The gradient signal used to fit the parameters has never passed through the selection step. This means the parameters were learned under the assumption that the correct answer is supplied at every step, while at generation time the model must pick and continue on its own. The mismatch between those two regimes is a named phenomenon in the literature: **exposure bias** — at inference the model is progressively exposed to an input distribution it never saw in training, built up from its own small errors.

Second, about whether the model can decline to choose. Ideally, if the current position needs no particular focus — a sentence has finished, what follows is boilerplate — the model should be able to *abstain*, rather than forcing attention onto some position that may not matter. But standard attention requires the weights over all candidate positions to sum to exactly 1 (the normalization requirement), which means the model **must** allocate weight somewhere, even when nothing deserves it. Researchers have verified three different repairs — adding a learnable bias to the denominator, providing a dedicated null slot pinned to zero, or multiplying the output by a gate that can approach zero — three implementations with nothing in common that achieve the same effect: the model gains the ability to turn in a blank ballot. This shows the problem is not the normalization rule but the absence, from the candidate set, of an option that can play the role of "choose nothing." As with a secret ballot: if there is no *abstain* line on the paper, everyone must vote for some candidate whether they want to or not, and the fix is to print the abstain line, not to abolish the requirement to fill the ballot.

The last detail concerns computing once and reusing later — and a small contradiction in AR that has never been fully articulated. As noted, the KV cache lets a model generate long text without recomputing everything for each new token: the already-computed part is stored, and the new token only needs its own relation to that store. What makes this legitimate is that the causal mask guarantees the already-written stretch has a computation result that never changes, whatever gets appended. That is a relatively weak guarantee: **prefix preservation**. There is a stronger one, **concatenation preservation**: joining two passages and feeding them in should give exactly the result of processing each separately and joining the outputs. AR satisfies the first and not the second — which is the root of the broader family of problems behind token healing: the same passage of text may be tokenized or processed differently depending on where it appears (standing alone, or appended to something else), a family of phenomena collectively called the **prompt boundary effect**. The KV cache works because prefix preservation holds; the prompt boundary effect exists because concatenation preservation does not. These two have historically been treated as separate engineering details, but they are **two strength levels of one structural regularity** — the weak level holding is what makes caching legitimate; the strong level failing is what leaves the boundary effect behind.

### Case B: Vector Quantization — The Same Account Charged Twice, in Opposite Directions

Vector quantization (VQ) is a common device in image and audio generation. Train an encoder that compresses a picture or an audio clip into a continuous vector; prepare a learned **codebook** holding a fixed number of vectors, each with an index; replace the encoder's continuous output with the nearest codebook vector. That replacement is *quantization*. Afterwards the model need only remember the index, look up the corresponding vector, and hand it to a decoder to reconstruct the picture or audio.

The interesting step is the replacement itself: a continuous vector with potentially infinitely many possible values is pressed onto one fixed representative in the codebook. This is exactly what Chapter 1's term **commitment** describes — **selecting one fixed representative out of a region where it was originally undetermined which to choose**. One confusion to clear up: commitment is not the vague notion of "going from fuzzy to precise" or "from probabilistic to certain." Its precise meaning is that a region — continuous or discrete — originally held many possible candidates and has been compressed to one. A tokenizer facing a string, where several legal segmentations exist and one is chosen, is commitment of exactly the same kind; its candidate set is merely finite rather than a continuum.

VQ has a loss term named for this: **commitment loss**, which forces the encoder output to commit to staying close to the codebook vector it was assigned rather than drifting away. Its existence makes VQ the only case here that **writes the cost of this conversion into its own objective function and penalizes it** — an AR text model has no such mechanism and does not track how far the selected token sits from the possibilities it displaced.

But look closely at how that loss is written and you find a detail long treated as one thing that is actually two. In the VQ objective, the quantity "distance between encoder output and assigned codebook vector" appears **twice**, handled in two opposite ways: once pulling the codebook vector toward the encoder output (*let the codebook fit the data*), once pulling the encoder output toward the codebook vector (*let the data fit the codebook*), with the two directions separated during training by a stop-gradient — freezing one side while letting the other move. So "how far this conversion's cost is perceived and propagated into training" is not a binary perceived/not-perceived. One must ask which side is being pulled, the codebook or the data; the answers can differ entirely. This corrects an earlier, cruder judgment: it is not enough to say "VQ's conversion cost is visible to training"; one must add *visible to whom, acting in which direction*.

Beyond those two opposing pulls, codebook updates admit a **third channel** — the exponential moving average (EMA) variant: instead of gradients, update codebook vectors by accumulating a weighted average of the encoder outputs actually assigned to them. This channel bypasses gradient descent entirely yet still lets the codebook track the encoder's output distribution. So "has this conversion's cost been perceived by the learning process" needs at least three cases: gradient pulling the codebook, gradient pulling the data, and non-gradient statistical update. Three entirely different implementations with similar effects on whether the codebook ends up adapting to the data.

One open item in VQ, left as a hook for Chapter 7. A codebook may contain **dead codes** — indices to which no encoder output was ever assigned, from the start of training to the end. Their existence is unsurprising in itself, but it threatens the foundation of the whole conversion account. Chapter 5 shows that the entire theory of whether a conversion loses information rests on a structural assumption: **every codebook vector, if fed back through the encoder and quantized again, should quantize back to itself.** For a code never actually used, nobody knows whether that holds. Set the suspense aside for now.

### One Gap, Appearing Twice

While working through VQ, a small but separable problem surfaced. The notation VQ uses — score, select an anchor, hand the anchor onward — assumes a **single** set of anchors: the anchors used for scoring are the same vectors as the one handed onward. But switch to a language model with untied weights (scoring vectors and output lookup table are different tables, a common engineering configuration), and the notation immediately falls short: scoring uses one set, the handoff another, and the notation has only one slot. Two sets of anchors cannot be written down.

More curiously, the same gap reappears in an unrelated case — the protein structure model AlphaFold2 in Case C — in a different form: one conversion there depends simultaneously on **two different partitions** (detailed below), and again the notation has only one slot. Two unrelated fields, with entirely different reasons, hit the same "the notation cannot hold this" gap at once. Chapter 3 writes the fix into the formalism.

### Case C: Diffusion Models and Protein Structure Prediction — Paying in Installments, and Seeing a Different World Through a Different Ruler

A diffusion model (commonly used for image generation) works quite unlike the previous two cases. It does not convert a continuous vector into a discrete label in one step; it starts from pure random noise and, over many steps of denoising, gradually shapes that noise into a clear image. Each step's output is still a continuous tensor of the same shape as its input — think of an image whose resolution is fixed but whose content grows sharper — involving no discrete index or codebook. So a single denoising step **does not** constitute a continuous-to-discrete conversion of the kind described above: it stays entirely inside the continuous world, and there is nothing to "select a representative" from, because there is no batch of candidate representatives at all.

The conversion actually comparable to the earlier cases happens at the **last** step: every pixel must end up as an integer between 0 and 255 (the standard image storage format), while the model computes a continuous value precise to many decimal places. Assigning that continuous value to one of 256 integer bins is the real conversion. Interestingly, when the model measures how well that assignment went during training, it does not compute "how far the continuous value is from the bin center" — it **integrates over the whole bin interval**. Every continuous value that could fall in the bin is included in the measurement; the bin center does not stand in for the bin. This is the exact opposite of VQ, where the codebook vector collapses to one fixed point and everything else inside the cell becomes invisible. Here the information inside the bin is **not discarded at all**; it is merely repackaged as an integral.

Which brings out a more general question: **after the conversion, can downstream computation still see the possibilities that were compressed away?** The answer differs entirely by case. In AR, not at all — the unselected candidates never even get looked up. In VQ it depends who receives the compressed-away possibilities: the part that generates the final image or audio cannot see them, but the part that measures how well the compression went can. The last step of a diffusion model sees them completely, because it integrates over the bin rather than sampling its center. Chapter 5 gives this question a name and shows it is tightly bound to the theory's central criterion.

A chained, stepwise conversion (multi-step denoising) has one direct advantage over a one-shot, irrevocable one (AR selecting a token): **later steps can correct the deviation left by earlier ones.** A one-shot conversion compresses away everything it is going to compress in a single act, with no way back if it compresses badly; a stepwise conversion gives up only part of the possibilities at each step, leaving the rest open for subsequent steps to adjust within. This explains why gradually amortizing a decision along a chain of steps feels more relaxed than going all at once — not because it is "smoother" in some vague aesthetic sense, but because it genuinely preserves room for correction in its mathematical structure. The price is equally direct: every step must recompare and recompute, so more steps mean more total computation.

Now for the conversion inside AlphaFold2, which is entirely unlike diffusion and equally instructive.

When AlphaFold2 predicts a protein's three-dimensional structure, it uses a **hierarchical** internal description: relatively coarse quantities describing the backbone's course and the rotation angles of key chemical bonds on each residue, from which it then **composes the precise three-dimensional coordinates of every atom**. That composition uses one crucial simplification: within each residue type, the bond lengths and bond angles between atoms are replaced by *ideal values* from the literature, rather than the slightly different real values each residue actually has. The genuinely molecule-specific micro-deformations of chemical bonds vanish entirely from this step's output; the coordinates reflect only where an idealized geometry lands when placed according to the predicted angles.

Here is the most instructive part of this case: **the same conversion, measured with a different ruler, yields an entirely different answer.** The model uses two evaluation metrics of different granularity: a coarser one, attending only to backbone course and residue center positions, used in intermediate training layers on the grounds that compute is limited and full precision is not needed everywhere; and a finer one, comparing the coordinates of every atom including side chains, used only at the final output layer. The same idealization is completely invisible to the coarse ruler (which does not care about side-chain atom positions) and clearly measurable by the fine one. **Change the ruler and the world changes** — not a contradiction or a hole in the theory, but a regularity that runs through the whole account: how much a conversion costs is never determined by the conversion alone; it depends on the pairing of the conversion's result with the ruler used to measure it.

The same regularity is corroborated in an entirely different field — image generation. One study deliberately removed the attention-sink positions from a diffusion image model. Measured by standard text-image alignment metrics, almost nothing changed; but looking at the generated images directly, the layout, palette, viewpoint, and style changed noticeably. The same deletion costs almost nothing by the semantic-matching ruler and a great deal by the pixel-rendering ruler. This is the same fact as the AlphaFold2 case, independently confirmed: different papers measure different downstream uses, so each sees a different picture. Not a conflict between studies — they have been measuring different faces of the same object all along.

### Case D: Retrieval-Augmented Generation — Where the Retrieved Thing Was Never Actually Selected

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has a language model first retrieve several of the most relevant documents from a large corpus, then splice their contents into the context for generating an answer.

RAG differs from every previous case in one respect: on the question of how to use the retrieved candidates, it **never makes a commitment** of the kind described above. Having retrieved the top *k* documents, it does not then pick one; it keeps each document's relevance weight and marginalizes over all *k* when generating — summing all candidate cases weighted by their probabilities rather than taking one. Within the retrieved *k*, **every member of the candidate set is retained and used in full; none is discarded.** This is quite unlike AR, where selecting a token erases every other candidate from downstream computation. RAG is more like convening five specialists and weighting all their opinions into the final judgment, rather than choosing one and discarding the other four.

This makes RAG the only known instance of a conversion that **makes no commitment at all inside its candidate set** — direct proof that commitment is not mandatory in every conversion: there exists a legitimate conversion in which the act degenerates to never being invoked.

Outside the candidate set, the situation is entirely different. Retrieval keeps only the top *k*; document *k+1* and everything after it is excluded from the computation altogether — and *that* is the irreversible step in this case. Precisely: **inside the top k, no commitment whatsoever; outside it, total discard with no remedy.**

RAG has another feature bearing on whether knowledge can be updated. In AR and VQ, all addresses (vocabulary entries, codebook vectors) are part of the model's parameters; adding a new address requires changing the parameter structure itself (there are dedicated functions for resizing the vocabulary embedding layer). RAG is different: the corpus it retrieves from is not in the parameters at all, but an external, independent dataset. To make the model "know" a new fact, add a document to the corpus — no retraining needed. The original paper advertises exactly this: swap the retrieval index and the model's world knowledge is refreshed, with no additional training.

That freedom to swap and the "no commitment at all" property are **two faces of the same design choice**. Precisely because no particular document's content is burned into the parameters, the corpus can be replaced at will; and precisely because the corpus is replaceable, the identity of any single document matters little to the model, so there is no need to force a choice of "the one correct" document. But the freedom is not free: the paper notes that this flexibility requires the encoder that produces document vectors to be **frozen** — if the encoder changed with training, the prebuilt index would have to be rebuilt, at a cost that is not worth paying. So **"the retrieval corpus can be swapped at any time," "the retriever encoder is frozen and no longer trained," and "this part of the computation can be safely cached and reused"** look like three different angles — one about replacing content, one about continuing training, one about caching — but are **three descriptions of the same underlying mechanism**, not three independent facts.

One subtler point deserves separate mention: **whether a set of candidate addresses can be swapped without retraining** and "what a given piece of content was converted into under one specific address scheme" are questions at different levels. The former asks whether one can jump from one scheme to another; the latter asks what the conversion yields within the current scheme. Keep the distinction in mind; Chapter 7 takes it up formally.

### Case E: Registers, `[MASK]`, Attention Sinks, Padding — The Positions That Were Never in the Signal

The last case is not one specific model but a phenomenon found across nearly every mainstream architecture.

Vision models use a device called a **register**: before an image is cut into patches and fed in, a number of extra "blank" positions are added, carrying no pixel information of their own, purely a scratchpad the model may use to store intermediate results as it pleases. Similar devices abound: the `[MASK]` token used in masked language model training to hide a word and have the model guess it; the attention-sink position discussed in Case A, produced structurally by the causal mask; the `padding` token filling out sentences of unequal length to a common length.

These positions share an easily missed feature: **nothing in the original input signal corresponds to them.** No character in a passage of text is naturally `[MASK]`; no patch of an image is naturally a `register`. Everything said earlier about the identity face and the similarity face concerned the content of the original signal itself; these special positions are bolted on from outside, not a member of any partition the original content produces. This "bolted on" property directly explains why they behave oddly — no further explanation is needed.

There is a further, finer distinction worth stating on its own: **what mechanism causes a slot to be selected or to form** and **what content that slot ends up holding once formed** are two entirely separate questions, not to be conflated. Detailed work on such slots in vision models has found at least two distinct types. The first carries almost no substantive content (by a technical measure of "how strong" its output is, its norm is far smaller than other positions at the same layer); its role is purely to *abstain* — the blank ballot from Case A. The second is entirely different: its output has a norm comparable to normal positions, and regardless of the input or the query, it outputs almost the same fixed vector every time — behavior that looks more like a global broadcast unrelated to the specific query than an abstention. These two types have different mechanisms and should not be lumped together; it is not accurate to say all such special positions exist "for abstaining."

A study of text-only language models gives a more direct quantitative piece of evidence. Researchers trained a range of differently structured models and measured the proportion of content positions (as opposed to dedicated special positions) pressed into service as attention sinks. In standard architectures this proportion runs roughly ten to forty-something percent, depending on model scale; once a learnable, content-free "abstain" option was added, that proportion collapsed to near zero, and the new dedicated abstain position absorbed nearly all of that demand, with almost no effect on overall training performance (validation loss essentially unchanged). This cleanly shows: **when a model lacks a dedicated abstain candidate, it falls back to conscripting some ordinary content position to fill in** — and doing so is not free. Once a genuinely dedicated abstain option exists, content positions no longer need to be conscripted this way. This also refines the judgment from Case A: the proportion of content positions conscripted was only ever a lower-bound proxy for a real demand for abstention, and once abstention is made free (a dedicated option exists), the real demand is fully revealed.

## Chapter 3: A Skeleton Emerges — Fitting the Five Cases into One Notation

Five cases now on the table; time to fold them back together and try to describe all five, on the surface unrelated, mechanisms with a single **shared notation**. The point is not elegance — it lets researchers from different fields check each other's findings against the same vocabulary, the way different engineering teams draw circuit diagrams with the same symbols instead of guessing what each other's boxes mean.

### A Tokenizer, It Turns Out, Defines "What Counts as Equal"

Start with a claim that sounds ordinary but is illuminating from this angle: **"designing a tokenizer" is equivalent to "stipulating, over the world of the raw signal, a rule for deciding whether two pieces count as the same thing."**

Start with a claim that sounds ordinary but is illuminating from this angle: **designing a tokenizer is equivalent to stipulating, over the world of the raw signal, a rule for deciding whether two pieces count as the same thing.**

The claim sounds convoluted at first but unpacks plainly. What a tokenizer ultimately does is map the vast raw content (every possible string, every possible pixel arrangement) onto a finite batch of addresses (the entries in a vocabulary). That mapping is, in essence, drawing lines of equivalence across the world of raw content: whatever gets mapped to the same address is, by this rule, judged the same thing. The address set (the vocabulary) is not something given in advance; it is the result of this equivalence rule; in the field this is called a quotient, from the mathematical notion of a quotient set: partition a large set by an equivalence relation, and each resulting group becomes one element of a smaller set.

The value of this view: it makes the whole theory independent of which model architecture is used. The theory only needs to talk about what the equivalence rule looks like, and that rule lives in the world of the raw signal, with no bearing on which neural network was trained on it. In other words, the theory talks about what a tokenizer does, not about how a particular tokenizers code happens to be written.

### The Seven-Part Signature

Gathering up the recurring elements from the five cases yields a fixed seven-part checklist. Any single continuous-discrete conversion can, in principle, be filled in against these seven slots:

1. The world of raw content, plus one specially bolted-on position standing for nothing at all (this is the formal root of the register / MASK-style slots from Case E; they correspond precisely to this extra position, not to any member of the raw contents own partition).
2. The address set, the quotient produced by the equivalence rule above.
3. The space carrying similarity, the continuous space where embedding vectors live.
4. The comparison function, used to measure how alike or how much two things should be grouped together: an inner product, a Euclidean distance, anything that ranks candidates without needing to be a mathematically strict metric (no requirement of symmetry or the triangle inequality).
5. The anchor map, the representative each address corresponds to in the continuous space: in AR, each vocabulary entrys embedding vector; in VQ, each codebook entry; in attention, each key vector.
6. The selection function, the one thing in charge of which representative this conversion ultimately picks. This is the only genuinely free part of the seven; the weight of that claim is unpacked further below.
7. A binary flag recording whether this conversion starts from the discrete or from the continuous side, detailed in the next chapter.

Together these seven make up what this article will call the conversion signature. It is not a newly invented, complicated formula; it is closer to a checklist. Facing any specific implementation of a continuous-discrete conversion, fill in the seven slots one by one; wherever a slot cannot be filled marks exactly the part of that implementation that has not yet been made explicit.

The first six of the seven describe what this conversion interface looks like: how many addresses, where the anchors sit, how likeness is compared. Only the sixth, the selection function, describes what the interface does; once the other six are fixed, whether a given conversion actually happens, and which address it lands on, is still entirely undetermined until the selection function makes the call. This is why Chapter 1 singled out the word commitment: commitment is the moment the selection function is actually invoked and makes its call.

With the seven-part signature in hand, Case Bs claim that commitment is not going from fuzzy to precise can be stated more precisely: what commitment really describes is selecting one representative from a batch of candidates that were, until then, left open. A tokenizer facing a string, with several legal segmentations available, is the finite-candidate case; VQ facing a continuous region with infinitely many possible landing points is the continuum case. The only difference between the two is the size of the candidate set, finite versus continuum, nothing else about what happens differs. This also means commitment need not require a continuous region to be present at all: Case Ds knowledge-graph-style retrieval and conversion purely among discrete addresses is genuine commitment too, with no continuous vector involved.

### Conversion Interfaces Always Come in Pairs

Once the seven-part signature is fixed, one structural fact falls out that had not been explicitly stated before: a standard language model performs not one continuous-discrete conversion but two, in opposite directions.

On the input side: raw text, itself discrete characters, is cut into discrete tokens (this direction runs from discrete content toward continuous representation; the original text is discrete, and after segmentation each token is translated into a continuous embedding vector for downstream computation). On the output side: the model computes continuous scores, which must ultimately be converted into one specific, selected discrete word (the opposite direction, from continuous representation toward a discrete result).

These two conversions use two entirely independent address sets. A well-known engineering practice, that the input vocabulary and output vocabulary can be set to different sizes with no coupling between them, had not previously been derived directly from this notation. Once "conversions come in pairs, running opposite directions" is recognized, this practice stops being an isolated engineering trick and becomes a direct consequence of the framework: since input and output each correspond to an independent conversion, their address sets can naturally be sized independently, with no further assumption needed to justify it.

The VQ case can also be reread this way: the encoder side runs from continuous toward discrete (quantizing a continuous vector into a codebook index); the decoder side runs from discrete toward continuous (translating the index back into a codeword vector, then reconstructing the image or audio). Again, two opposite conversions forming one complete round trip.

### The One Slot With Real Freedom

Of the seven, only the selection function has real freedom, as noted above. Worth dwelling on, because it resurfaces in an unexpected way in Chapter 6.

The other six, once a specific model architecture is fixed, are essentially fixed along with it; the size of the address set, where the anchors sit, how comparison is done, are all static parameters pinned down at design time. The selection function is different: it may deterministically pick the single top-scoring candidate (greedy decoding in AR always takes the highest score), or it may sample stochastically from a batch of candidates by probability (temperature sampling in AR, or a tokenizer randomly choosing among several legal segmentations). Randomness lives in this one place and nowhere else. This also explains a technical detail glossed over earlier: there is, in principle, such a thing as random tokenization, the same passage tokenized differently at different times, that still guarantees lossless round-tripping, provided the different segmentations correspond to non-overlapping partitions of the original content, so that whichever one gets picked, reconstruction is unaffected. The underlying mechanism is exactly this: randomness is placed in the selection function and nowhere else.

## Chapter 4: Which Face Is Native

Chapter 1 cast the token as a two-faced object, identity on one side and similarity on the other. This chapter asks a sharper question: of the two, which is the one "originally there," and which is "derived afterward"?

### Which Face Is Native Depends on How the Conversion Is Placed

The answer is somewhat counterintuitive: the question itself is aimed the wrong way. Which face is native is not an intrinsic property of the concept of a token; it depends entirely on how this particular conversion happens to be placed.

Take the most typical scenario, text tokenization for input: a string is, from the start, made of discrete characters (the ground floor of the identity face); a tokenizer cuts it into tokens, each of which is then translated into a continuous embedding vector. This flow runs from discrete to derived-continuous, so in this scenario the identity face is native and the similarity face is derived. AlphaFold2's hierarchical description in Case C (discrete residue types and angle parameters composing continuous three-dimensional coordinates) runs the same direction.

Now look at AR's output sampling and VQ's quantization: the model first computes a continuous vector (a hidden state, an encoder output), and from that continuous vector derives a discrete address. This flow runs the opposite way — the similarity face is native, the identity face is derived. The multi-step denoising in diffusion models from Case C is the same: it stays continuous throughout and involves no discrete intermediate step at all.

Retrieval from Case D is a special, two-sided case: retrieval can be done by continuous vector similarity (comparing likeness in a continuous space first) or by discrete knowledge-graph structure (querying discrete entity relations first). Both are used in practice; neither is more "native" than the other.

**This leads to an uncomfortable conclusion: text tokenization, the scenario everyone defaults to as the archetype of a token, is precisely the outlier among the placements surveyed.** Along with AlphaFold2's hierarchical description, it is one of only two "identity-native" scenarios; AR output, VQ, and diffusion, the three mainstream machine learning scenarios, are all "similarity-native"; retrieval stands as a third category on its own, with either pole viable and neither more native. This also answers, directly, where Chapter 1's opening claim — "a token is a slice of the signal" — comes from: **it comes precisely from this one identity-native placement.** Switch to a similarity-native placement, and the signal is not being "cut" at all; it is being **snapped** onto a pre-fixed anchor — slicing pulls a part out of a whole, snapping pulls a continuous point onto a discrete representative, opposite directions entirely. Chapter 1 left this as a suspended thread; here it is formally redeemed.

### The Two Placements Break in Opposite Directions

Conversion involves maps running two directions: one converts content into an address (tokenization, say), the other converts an address back into content (translating an index back into a specific string or vector). Ideally, converting then converting back should return exactly the original content, losslessly; and converting back then converting again should return exactly the original address.

In identity-native scenarios such as text tokenization: cutting and then reassembling (converting back) is completely lossless, as long as the segmentation scheme itself drops no characters, joining the pieces returns the exact original string. But the reverse, taking an address, converting it back to a string, then re-tokenizing that string, does not necessarily return the original address. This is the root of the token-healing phenomenon from the opening: the same string, tokenized on its own, may be cut differently than it was cut inside its original context.

In similarity-native scenarios such as VQ, it is the reverse: a codebook address, converted back to its codeword vector and then re-quantized, is guaranteed to return the same address, because a codeword vector, by construction, already sits at the point closest to itself, which is arithmetically necessary. But the reverse, taking an encoder actual continuous output, quantizing it to an address, then converting back to the codeword vector, will almost certainly not return exactly that original continuous vector. The gap produced here is precisely the quantity the commitment loss is measuring and penalizing.

The two placements break exactly the opposite direction round-trip guarantee. This asymmetry is one of two independent statements of the same fact noted in Case A: the map that converts an address back to content is a well-behaved homomorphism respecting concatenation, while segmentation itself is not. One statement follows from asking what structure each capacity requires; the other follows from an algebraic path asking which composition holds. Two independent routes arriving at the same conclusion is a sign this is not coincidence but a structural product of the conversion scheme itself.

### A Sharper Question: Not "Same Coin," but "Does Downstream Still Need What Was Erased"

Chapter 1 used the metaphor of a two-faced object for identity and similarity, intuitive, but on closer inspection not quite accurate.

Line up the capacities assigned to the identity face (countable, indexable, reusable) and to the similarity face (comparable, interpolable) against the seven representative placements surveyed (the six cases above, plus text input), and a very clean symmetric pattern appears.

Take reusability, an identity-face capacity: across all seven placements, without exception, reusability holds at the address level and fails at the content level. A word embedding vector (the fixed representative an address maps to) is the same vector no matter which sentence it appears in, and can be reused without recomputation; but the hidden state that word actually produces in a specific context (what this instance of content actually looks like) varies with the surrounding text and cannot be reused.

Now take interpolability, a similarity-face capacity, and the pattern is nearly a mirror image: at the level of the original continuous carrier (say, the space in which two codeword vectors themselves live), interpolation always holds, the midpoint of two vectors is a legitimate point in that space; but once that midpoint is mapped back to the address level (which fixed representative does this midpoint belong to), interpolation generally fails, because the midpoint of two representatives is generally not itself a representative.

These two capacities exhibit exactly opposite patterns of success: reusability is alive at the address, dead at the content; interpolability is alive at the content, dead at the address. Such a neat, mirrored fit suggests the two-faced metaphor does not hold up: these two capacities are not really properties of two separate, independent, parallel faces, they are byproducts of the two sides of one and the same act (merging continuous content into discrete addresses). The act of partitioning erases context-level difference wholesale, and that very erasure is what grants the address level its reusability (differences are erased, so everything looks the same everywhere, safe to reuse); the same erasure is also what strips interpolability away at the address level (differences are erased, so the intermediate states go with them). One is a gain from the partition, the other a loss from the same partition, occurring on the two sides of a single coin, better described as occurring on the two sides of the cut the partitioning act makes, rather than on two faces of one coin.

This naturally yields a sharper question. Chapter 1 originally asked when identity and similarity are, in fact, the same coin, a phrasing that quietly assumes the two faces are inherently comparable, that it makes sense to ask whether they are equal. The lesson here is that the more precise question is: does the downstream computation actually still need the differences this partition erased? If not, no matter how aggressively this partition erases, it costs nothing; if so, the partition has incurred a real cost. Chapter 5 turns this sentence into a computable quantity.

One aside. Interpolability, on any finite set of addresses (the vocabularies and codebooks in every mainstream scenario), is almost bound to fail, the midpoint of two representatives is, as a rule, unlikely to also be a representative; this is an arithmetic fact of finite sets, not an empirically contingent regularity. The one exception is AlphaFold2 from Case C: it depends on two different kinds of partitions at once, one over finitely many residue types (interpolation necessarily fails there) and one over continuously valued rotation angles (interpolation holds completely there, because an angle is naturally a continuous circle, and its midpoint is naturally legitimate). This exception is exactly what confirms the earlier notation gap: the same placement can depend on more than one kind of partition at once, which is exactly what the seven-part signature in Chapter 3 still cannot write down.

### "This Capacity Holds in This Scenario" Is Not Yet Precise Enough

Working through the list of capacities above surfaces a further methodological lesson: saying only that a given capacity holds or fails in a given scenario is not informative enough on its own, and can mislead.

The reason: different capacities can split along different axes. Interpolability splits along content level versus address level; reusability splits along the same axis, only with success and failure reversed; but composability (from Chapter 1, can it be assembled like building blocks) splits along an entirely different axis, the direction of the mapping. The direction that converts an address back to content obeys the composition rule properly (converting two pieces back separately and joining them equals joining first and then converting back); the direction that converts content into an address does not obey it (this is the root of the prompt boundary effect).

In other words, within the same capacity times scenario table, different rows are not even asking the same kind of question, some ask content level or address level, others ask which direction of mapping. Writing only holds or fails, without also stating which layer and which direction is meant, leaves the statement underdetermined; a reader has no way to actually verify what it means. This looks like a mere formatting detail for filling in a table, but it points to something larger: the vocabulary this theory uses to describe capacities is not yet fine enough, every capacity needs two further tags, side (content level or address level) and direction (the round-trip direction or the conversion direction), before the claim can be stated precisely. This problem recurred several times over the course of the project, suggesting it is not a one-off oversight but a systemic symptom of a vocabulary that has not yet fully matured.

## Chapter 5: The Fixed-Point Dream, and Its Collapse

The previous four chapters covered phenomena, cases, and notation. This chapter takes a step toward the theoretical core: can a general criterion be found, one glance at which tells whether a given conversion has its two faces well aligned? This chapter tells the story of one ambitious attempt at such a criterion, how it was corrected step by step by reality, and how a more precise but also more restricted replacement eventually grew out of the wreckage.

### The Original Bet: A Round Trip Back to Where It Started

The most intuitive way to measure whether a conversion is good: convert content into an address, then convert the address back into content; if that round trip returns exactly the starting point, the conversion lost no information. This returning to the same place after a full loop property has a name in mathematics: a fixed point, the starting point, after the round trip, lands back exactly where it began, without moving.

The idea does hold up in the simplest scenario: if a segmentation scheme is well designed (unambiguous, every cut uniquely determined), cutting a passage of text into tokens and reassembling those tokens returns the original text exactly, with no loss. This already-proven special case naturally became the foundation the theory first wanted to lean on, generalize it into a criterion applicable to any conversion, and the whole theory would have a solid footing.

But this bet quickly ran into two problems.

The first: the coverage of this already-proven special case is narrower than it looked. What it actually proves is a round trip between two discrete address sets of different granularity, a string is itself a discrete address set too, just at a finer granularity than tokens. In other words, the special case proves a round trip inside the identity face, between two levels of fineness, and never touches a round trip between the identity face and the similarity face, which is exactly the core question this theory actually wants to cover. This crack is wider than first assumed.

The second problem is more lethal: once the fixed-point requirement is actually generalized to the similarity-native placements (AR sampling in Case A, VQ in Case B), it turns out the requirement can never hold, not merely difficult to satisfy, but structurally false from the mathematics. The reason is simple: the first step of any such conversion presses a continuous distribution down onto a handful of discrete anchors, and unless the original distribution happened to already be concentrated exactly on those anchors (which, in reality, almost never happens), this pressing-down act necessarily changes the shape of the distribution. Demanding before pressed down equal after pressed down is like demanding a lump of clay, once flattened, keep exactly the same shape it had before flattening, not bad luck at failing to flatten it right, but a demand that cannot logically be satisfied in the first place.

The fixed-point bet lost. But it lost in a way that had real value: it pointed precisely at which direction to look for a replacement. If demanding absolutely nothing changes is doomed, then relax the requirement to certain specific quantities, the ones downstream actually uses, should stay the same before and after the pressing-down. Mathematically, this corresponds to something called weak convergence or moment matching: do not require the two distributions to be identical pointwise, only require them to agree on the averages of a batch of test functions.

Here, test functions means exactly what downstream actually does with the result, which statistics it reads, which decisions it makes. The set of these test functions is denoted throughout by one symbol, Phi, understood as the collection of things downstream actually cares about.

### Phi Must Be Declared in Advance, Never Picked After the Fact

The idea of Phi carries a hidden danger that must be closed off first, or the whole theory collapses on the spot: if it is allowed to look at the conversion result first and then reach back to pick a batch of test functions that happen to make the result look loss-free, the criterion means nothing at all. Any conversion, if picking the measuring standard after seeing the result is allowed, can always have a standard picked for it that makes it look flawless, a theory that can always be shown correct however it is defined has said nothing.

The fix is a hard rule: Phi must be declared before any measurement, and it may only be read directly off the architecture itself, never picked backward from the result. Read off the architecture is an operation that can be verified, independent of any researcher opinion: Phi is simply the set of quantities this conversion output will actually be read and used by the next step of computation, determined by looking at the code and the model structure, with no bearing on what the researcher wants to prove.

An example: in AR, what the next step of computation actually receives is the embedding vector of the selected token, and beyond that nothing but a handful of linear transforms of that vector and their inner products. Phi is exactly that, nothing else. This rule immediately yields a somewhat sharp conclusion: in AR, once the conversion is done, downstream computation has absolutely no way to detect any difference between the selected token and the candidates that were not selected, because what downstream reads is only a function of the one that was selected, entirely independent of what else was a candidate at the time, and how likely each one was. This means the entire cost of this conversion in AR cannot show up in some hidden detail still buried inside the selected token, it can only show up in the act of selecting this one and giving up all the rest. This sharp conclusion is put to use later, in the diagnosis section of this chapter.

### A Theorem: Zero Cost Is Exactly Equivalent to the Differences Among These Candidates Never Being Noticed Downstream Anyway

Writing the idea of Phi into strict mathematical language yields a fairly clean theorem: this conversion cost is exactly zero if and only if every test function in Phi depends only on the address itself, never on the details that got pressed away. In other words, as long as downstream computation simply does not care which specific content got converted to this address, and cares only about which address it landed on, then the conversion is entirely free, it causes no downstream-detectable loss whatsoever.

This theorem has one property worth emphasizing on its own: it is entirely independent of the actual data distribution. Whether the criterion holds depends only on what downstream cares about, and has nothing whatsoever to do with what the data fed to the model actually looks like. This is almost a restatement, in another form, of the earlier rule that Phi must be declared in advance and never picked after the fact, the data itself cannot produce any tricks to change whether this conversion holds or fails; everything is decided by what was fixed in advance as downstream concerns.

This also resolves an observation left open in Case C: why, across so many different conversion scenarios, does the Phi measuring what downstream cares about so often turn out to look identical on close inspection? The answer: these scenarios have similar Phi precisely because their Phi all happen to fall inside the safe zone of depends only on the address, not on content detail. What actually widens the gap between scenarios is never Phi itself, but whether something else exists that can pierce through this safe zone and re-expose the details that were pressed away, which echoes exactly the recurring question from Cases B and C about whether downstream computation can see what got pressed away. This theorem formally ties the two together into one fact: whether the pressed-away details can be seen determines, directly, whether this conversion cost is exactly zero.

### The Criterion Reports Zero on AR, and AR Plainly Does Pay a Cost — the Criterion Fails Its Own Test

At this point the theory owes itself an honest self-check: can this criterion, on a scenario **already known to be problematic**, correctly report a problem? If a criterion always says everything is fine, it is equivalent to saying nothing, with no power to discriminate.

Try it on AR. By the Phi-declaration rule above, what downstream actually cares about in AR is only a function of the selected token embedding vector, and strictly speaking, every such function depends only on the address, never on any detail that was pressed away. By the theorem in the previous section, this means: **the criterion, applied to AR, will report a cost of exactly zero.**

But that conclusion is plainly absurd. Tokenization and sampling clearly do carry a cost; the token-healing phenomenon from the opening is real, acknowledged engineering fact. The criterion reporting zero, as an answer to the narrower question "can downstream actually detect a difference," is entirely correct — downstream genuinely cannot; but as a theory meant to measure cost, reporting "no problem at all" at exactly the point where it should raise an alarm is a genuine failure of the theory.

**This test did not pass.** But the manner of the failure is informative: it is not a clumsy failure where the criterion holds everywhere and has lost all power to discriminate — it fails precisely at the one specific scenario it should have flagged. This is a failure that can be pinned down exactly and repaired, not a vague collapse.

### Diagnosis: One Conversion Hides Two Different Kinds of Collapse, and the Criterion Only Measures One

Following this failure further uncovers a structural issue that had gone unnoticed until now: a standard conversion actually does **two** things at once, and the existing criterion measures only one of them.

The first thing is "pressing content down onto an address" — compressing an entire continuous region onto one fixed anchor, say. What gets pressed away in this step is **the specific difference among the candidates this particular conversion cares about**. This step is exactly what the theorem in the previous section measures.

The second thing is a completely different kind of collapse: **out of the entire probability distribution over all candidate addresses, only the one selected is kept, and every other candidate, along with its probability weight, is thrown away outright.** This step the previous theorem **does not measure at all** — because that theorem compares "the distribution before pressing" to "the distribution after pressing," a comparison that already presupposes which one was selected has already happened, settled, without ever going back to ask what the other candidates were at the time, and what weight each carried.

**This is the real reason AR looked like an outlier** — not, as Chapter 2 once suspected, "a difference in spaces" (that judgment has already been overturned), but a **difference in what kind of cost is being incurred**: nearly all of AR cost falls on this second kind of collapse, while the existing criterion measures only the first. VQ looks, by contrast, as if "its architecture has already accounted for its own cost," because its commitment loss happens to measure exactly the first kind of collapse (the specific gap between the encoder output and the codebook representative) — squarely within what the criterion can cover. And the formula from Case C comparing "the average candidate" against "the one selected candidate," once thought to be merely a special case of the criterion, is now clear: **it is precisely the second term the criterion was missing all along, not a variant of the first.**

### Adding the Missing Second Term

Having diagnosed a whole missing category, the fix is straightforward: add the second term honestly. Beyond measuring the cost of "pressing content down," add a separate term measuring specifically the cost of "keeping only one out of a whole weighted batch of candidates" — concretely, comparing "what would result from averaging all candidates by their respective weights" against "what was actually selected," and measuring the gap between the two.

This second term has one clean property: it does **not** automatically vanish under the same "downstream only cares about the address" exemption — because "the virtual candidate produced by weighted averaging" is, almost certainly, not equal to any real candidate that actually exists, so even when downstream test functions depend only on the address, the gap between "the averaged candidate" and "the actually selected candidate" is still detectable. This means the added second term has its own exemption region, entirely different from the first — neither term can substitute for the other, and the two cannot be merged into one.

This two-term, upgraded criterion checks out against several cases: in retrieval, Case D, since no genuine selection ever happens (recall the "never truly selects" property), the second term is naturally zero; the last step of a diffusion model no longer samples but displays the model computed mean directly, so no genuine selection happens there either, and the second term is again zero; AR is exactly the opposite — its first term is zero, its second term strictly positive. Four situations — retrieval, the last diffusion step, AR, and VQ from Case B — give four different combinations under this two-term criterion, and each cross-checks the others.

### The Criterion Degenerates to Empty Talk Unless Phi Is Declared First

One last methodological point deserves emphasis. Unpacked, this whole "measuring cost" mathematical apparatus is not, on its own, any newly invented mathematical tool — using a batch of test functions to measure the gap between two distributions is an established, mature technique in statistics and probability theory. **As a piece of mathematics, this criterion has nothing novel about it at all**; its entire, sole substance rests on one single question: how is Phi actually decided?

Choosing Phi is choosing which ruler to measure with; and if that ruler can be picked backward from the result, then any conversion at all can always have a ruler picked for it that makes it look entirely loss-free. The earlier rule — Phi must be declared in advance, read only from the architecture — is the **sole line of defense** keeping this whole criterion from degenerating into empty talk. This is also why Case C observation about "a different ruler seeing a different world" was more than a passing curiosity — it is the very thing this criterion life depends on.

## Chapter 6: The Hinge the Criterion Cannot Reach

The two-term criterion from the previous chapter looks fairly thorough by now — it measures the cost of compressing content, and the cost of the selection act itself. But there remains a whole class of question this criterion is **structurally incapable of reaching**, not because the current version is not careful enough, but because this form of criterion has a built-in blind spot. This chapter explains exactly what that blind spot is, and how to live with it.

### The Criterion Measures How Much Distributions Differ; Some Questions Ask What the Mapping Itself Looks Like

First, why the blind spot exists at all. Both terms of the criterion, whichever one is applied, are fundamentally comparisons between **two probability distributions**: how much "the distribution before conversion" differs from "the distribution after," or how much "the averaged candidate" differs from "the selected candidate." This kind of comparison is, mathematically, an integral over a distribution, and integration has one property: **as long as the shape of the distribution itself is unchanged, no matter how the underlying conversion rule (which point maps to which point) is varied within some region almost never actually sampled, the integral comes out the same.** In statistical language, this kind of criterion depends only on the conversion rule almost-everywhere equivalence class, never on its exact value at a sparse handful of points.

And there happen to be three previously mentioned, important capacities that are, by nature, **not** the kind of property where "shape is right, detail does not matter":

The first is differentiability — whether a derivative can be taken, whether gradient descent can be used to optimize. This is a pointwise, local property; changing the mapping behavior around just one point can flip differentiability instantly, unaffected by how small the range being changed is or how rarely it gets sampled.

The second is cacheability — whether a conversion, once made, is irrevocable and can be safely stored and reused. This too is a precise property about the mapping local behavior.

The third is composability — whether converting two joined pieces of content equals converting each separately and then joining. This property concerns what happens at two specific points (not an entire distribution), likewise unrelated to anything in the sense of "overall shape."

**All three of these capacities are simply not the same kind of thing as the "only overall distribution shape matters" measure the criterion depends on.** So any criterion built around "comparing how much two distributions differ," however it is tuned, is structurally unable to detect trouble in these three capacities — not bad luck, not an oversight, but a necessary consequence of the very shape of this kind of criterion.

If this conclusion rests only on an abstract mathematical argument, it invites the objection that it is a contrived counterexample. So here is a more grounded, engineering-facing statement. By the previous chapter theorem, everything downstream cares about in AR depends only on the selected address, so the first term of the criterion is identically zero on AR. But in engineering practice, an entire dedicated subfield has grown up around the question of "how to make this act of selection differentiable" — straight-through estimators, REINFORCE, Gumbel-Softmax relaxation — and the **sole reason** any of these techniques exist is that "selection" is, by its nature, not differentiable; the gradient cannot flow through it, so a substitute gradient has to be manufactured. The existence of this entire subfield is itself the strongest possible counterexample: the criterion rules AR "entirely free," while AR is exactly the source of an entire subfield worth of engineering trouble. **On the single largest engineering difficulty here, the criterion answers zero.** Not a matter of insufficient precision — this kind of criterion simply lacks the dimension along which that difficulty could ever be measured.

### Three Capacities, Really the Same Sentence Repeated Across Three Different Structures

These three capacities the criterion cannot reach, differentiable, cacheable, composable, look unrelated on the surface, but rephrased from a different angle they turn out to be asking the same question: does this conversion act preserve, intact, some piece of extra structure already present in the world of the original signal?

The world of the original signal is never just a bag of isolated points; it typically carries some extra structure of its own, strings can be concatenated end to end into longer strings (a concatenation structure); a continuous vector space lets one take a derivative at a point, move a small step in some direction (a smooth structure); a sequence has an order, earlier content and later content standing in a definite sequence (a sequential structure).

So the three capacities can be rewritten in one unified way: composability asks whether the conversion preserves the concatenation structure intact; differentiability asks whether it preserves the smooth structure intact; cacheability asks whether it preserves the sequential structure intact, specifically, whether the current step result depends only on what has already happened before it, and cannot be secretly rewritten by something that has not happened yet.

All three collapse into one sentence: does this conversion act preserve some given extra structure? And that sentence also explains an oddity noted earlier: Chapter 1 observed that the direction converting an address back to content properly obeys concatenation (converting two pieces back separately and joining equals joining first then converting back), while the direction converting content into an address does not obey it (the root of the prompt boundary effect). Seen through this unified does it preserve structure lens, that asymmetry stops looking strange at all: the return direction preserves structure by construction; the conversion direction, by construction, does not. The behavioral difference between the two directions is a necessary, directional consequence built into the system itself, not two separate observations each needing its own explanation.

### The Hinge and the Criterion Measure Two Different Kinds of Quantity, and Cannot Be Merged Into One

Having found this unified language of does it preserve structure, a natural next thought arises: can that idea also be written as a concrete formula and merged with the two terms of the earlier criterion into one larger, more elegant, more unified equation?

Actually trying this shows it does not work, and it fails cleanly: what the hinge asks and what the criterion asks are, formally, two fundamentally different kinds of quantity, and there is no way to merge them into one equation.

The criterion (the two terms in Chapter 5) measures a kind of displacement, how far a point, after being converted and converted back, ends up from where it started. This is the gap between one point and its own image, involving only a single path.

The hinge, by contrast, is concerned with a kind of commutator: the same input, taken along two different paths, how much do the final results differ. Take convert then concatenate versus concatenate then convert, two different paths, and the hinge asks how far apart these two paths end up. This requires two paths to compare in the first place before commutator even makes sense; a displacement quantity has no second path at all, do nothing, stay right where you are does not count as another way of doing it, there is no second path to compare it against.

Forcing displacement into the commutator framework would require inventing some operation under which doing nothing becomes the result of commuting with the conversion act, but no such operation can be constructed. So it is more honest to admit these are two different kinds of quantity than to force-fit them into one equation.

This means the original goal of one criterion says it all was not achieved, but the manner of not achieving it can be stated clearly, not left as a vague needs more research: this theory ultimately needs two different kinds of measuring tools for whether the two faces are properly aligned, one displacement criterion (the two terms from Chapter 5), plus a family of commutators (one dedicated commutator for each kind of extra structure, concatenation, smoothness, sequence, and so on). The only reason to insist on forcing the two into one formula would be aesthetic tidiness, with no mathematical necessity behind it.

### The One Instance With an Actual Formula: Strong and Weak Tiers on Sequential Structure

This commutator idea for the hinge remains, so far, only a unified framework. Of the four kinds of structure (measure, concatenation, smoothness, sequence), only sequential structure has actually been worked into a concrete formula; the others remain at the stage of knowing which direction to think in, with no computable formula yet in hand. This section covers the one instance that has been worked out, and along the way reports an interesting, unexpected finding: whether sequential structure is preserved is not, it turns out, a simple yes-or-no question, it splits into two tiers, strong and weak.

The weak tier is called prefix preservation: joining two pieces of content and converting them together should give the earlier piece exactly the same result as converting that piece alone, whatever gets appended afterward should not affect what has already been computed for the earlier part.

The strong tier is called concatenation preservation: converting two pieces separately and then joining the results should give exactly the same outcome as joining the pieces first and converting them together. The strong tier logically implies the weak one (if joining and converting together gives the same result throughout, then naturally the earlier stretch alone will not change either); but the reverse does not hold, satisfying only the weak tier does not mean the strong tier is also satisfied.

AR sits precisely between these two tiers. It satisfies the weak tier (the causal mask guarantees that once a stretch of content is written, its computed result will not be rewritten no matter what gets appended after it), and this is exactly the necessary and sufficient condition for the KV cache technique to legitimately work, computing the earlier content once and reusing it for everything that follows relies exactly on this guarantee that earlier results will not be rewritten by later content. But it does not satisfy the strong tier (processing two pieces separately and then joining gives a different result than joining and processing together), which is exactly the root of the prompt boundary effect.

These two tiers happen to explain two engineering phenomena previously treated as unrelated, one explains why the KV cache works, the other explains why the prompt boundary effect exists. These two have historically been discussed as two unconnected topics; seen this way, they turn out to be two instances of the same structure at two different threshold levels, not two separate coincidences that happen to occur together.

One aside: the same vocabulary, used to support both AR greedy decoding and a tokenizer segmentation, takes different values on this weak prefix preservation tier, AR, thanks to the hard guarantee of the causal mask, strictly satisfies prefix preservation; but the greedy longest-match algorithm commonly used by tokenizers can have later text change how earlier text gets segmented (another way of stating the token-healing phenomenon from the opening). This shows that this particular cost on sequential structure is a property of the conversion rule itself, not of some more basic property like how large the address set is or what it looks like, the same vocabulary, paired with a different conversion rule, incurs an entirely different cost.

### One Generalization Deliberately Not Made

At this point a tempting thought naturally arises: the strong-weak tier pattern on sequential structure is elegant enough, can it be directly transplanted onto other structures? Say, calling interpolability the weak tier and differentiability the strong tier, and getting the same neat two-tier story?

This temptation was deliberately not acted on, and the reason is worth stating, because it embodies a discipline worth learning: differentiability is a local condition (concerned only with behavior in a small neighborhood around a single point), while preserving convex combinations (interpolation) is a global condition (concerned with what happens along the entire line connecting two points that may be far apart). There is no containment relationship between these two kinds of property, a mapping can be affine (satisfying the stronger, global, linear condition) and still differentiable, but the reverse does not hold: a differentiable mapping need not satisfy the stronger, global condition of being affine. Since the two do not stand in the same strong implies weak containment relation as the sequential case, directly copying the strong-weak two tier pattern over would produce a conclusion that looks similar but does not actually hold up.

This record of deliberately not generalizing further is worth stating on its own: a pattern holding in one place does not mean it holds when transplanted elsewhere; noticing a pattern looks elegant is exactly the moment to go check where its boundary actually lies, rather than rushing to apply it everywhere.
