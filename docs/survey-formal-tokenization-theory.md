# Three Formal Theories of Tokenization: Round-Trip Consistency, Coding Efficiency, and Character-Level Surprisal

This note covers three papers that each try to put tokenization on a formal footing, from three different angles: whether the encoder-decoder round trip preserves the true distribution (Gastaldi et al., arXiv:2407.11606), whether the induced token-frequency distribution is efficient to learn from (Zouhar et al., arXiv:2306.16842), and how to correctly compute character-level probabilities for downstream tasks that never asked to be tokenized in the first place (Vieira et al., arXiv:2410.02691). Read together, they form a coherent stack: the first supplies the general algebra of the encoder/decoder round trip, the second asks whether the distribution that round trip induces is good to learn, and the third shows what happens when you actually need to undo the round trip for a purpose the tokenizer was never designed to serve.

These notes were written as literature background for the token theory in [The Antinomy of the Token](token-antinomy.md) and its companion notes on the conversion signature and the displacement criterion; where those notes' own "already-proven case" and "section property" draw on formal facts, this is where those facts come from.

## 1. Gastaldi, Cotterell, et al., "The Foundations of Tokenization" (arXiv:2407.11606)

### 1.1 The formal object

A tokenizer is modeled as a pair of stochastic maps: an encoder tau, mapping a character string (an element of Sigma-star) to a distribution over token strings (elements of Delta-star), and a decoder kappa, mapping a token string back to a distribution over character strings. Both are Markov kernels — total, normalized, and fixed (not learned, not varying with sample size). What actually controls the statistical outcome is not either map alone but the **round-trip kernel** K = kappa composed with tau, acting on distributions over Sigma-star.

If p-star is the true distribution over character strings, the induced token-level distribution is q-star = tau applied to p-star. If a token-level language model q_n converges to q-star (in the sense of pointwise convergence over a countable, normalized probability space, which the paper's Lemma 3.1 shows is equivalent to total-variation convergence — essentially a discrete Scheffe's lemma), then decoding q_n converges to K applied to p-star. So:

**kappa applied to q_n converges to p-star if and only if K applied to p-star equals p-star.**

This is a fixed-point condition on the round-trip kernel. **The paper's most accurate summary is not "tokenization is unbiased or not" but "the asymptotic bias introduced by tokenization equals however far K applied to p-star sits from p-star."** Calling a tokenizer **exact** (K = identity) is simply strengthening "unbiased for this particular p-star" into "unbiased for every possible distribution."

### 1.2 What exactness actually requires, and what it does not

If K applied to p equals p for *every* distribution p, then evaluating at every point-mass distribution immediately forces K to equal the identity map exactly (Proposition 3.1). And if kappa composed with tau equals the identity, then every token sequence the encoder actually uses for a given text x must be decoded by kappa, with probability 1, back to that same x (Proposition 3.2) — which forces the encoder's support sets for different texts to be pairwise disjoint. Tau is free to randomize *within* the legal segmentations of one text, but it cannot mix segmentations across different texts and remain exact.

This gives the complete structure of an exact tokenizer: the portion of Delta-star the encoder actually uses splits into fibers indexed by text; tau picks an arbitrary distribution within each text's own fiber; kappa deterministically maps each fiber back to its text. Outside the encoder's support, kappa can be defined however one likes — and this is exactly where spurious ambiguity, discussed below, enters.

**One distinction this note keeps returning to must be kept sharp: distributional consistency is not sample-wise losslessness.** K applied to p-star equals p-star only requires the aggregate distribution to be preserved, not that every individual input text be recoverable. A non-injective encoder can map several distinct texts to the same token sequence, and a stochastic decoder can then regenerate those texts randomly in proportion to their prior probabilities, exactly reproducing the marginal distribution of p-star — this counts as "consistent" under the paper's definition, yet it preserves no information about which specific text produced a given token sequence. Three distinct notions must therefore be told apart: **distributional consistency** (K applied to p equals p), **lossless coding / exact reconstruction** (every individual text round-trips to itself, i.e. K equals the identity), and **identifiability** (whether q-star alone determines p-star uniquely). If tau is not injective, there generally exist two different distributions p_1 and p_2 with the same tau-image, and no fixed decoder can recover both; distribution-relative consistency can paper over this with a known prior, but it does not resolve the underlying unidentifiability.

**A related terminological trap: "bijective BPE/WordPiece" is a restricted claim.** The paper calls a deterministic, exact tokenizer bijective, but kappa equals the inverse of tau only *on the encoder's canonical image*. Over the full space Delta-star, strings like `t|h|e`, `t|he`, and `th|e` can all decode to `the` under an ordinary concatenation decoder, so that decoder remains many-to-one globally. The correct statement is: encoder and its image are in bijection; the decoder can still produce alternative segmentations outside that image.

### 1.3 Ambiguity, unified

Character-level probability, in general, must be computed by summing over every token string that decodes to the target: p_Sigma(sigma) equals the sum, over every delta with kappa(delta) equal to sigma, of the token-level probability of delta. Only computing the probability of the canonical tokenization is not, in general, the character-level probability. The three kinds of ambiguity the paper distinguishes are unified by this: token-level mass is spread across multiple fiber elements over the same character text — a deterministic tokenizer's LM assigning mass outside the encoder's image produces **spurious ambiguity**; a stochastic segmentation scheme deliberately assigning mass to several legal segmentations produces **regularization ambiguity**; and giving token boundaries a linguistic reading produces **linguistic ambiguity**. The claim that ordinary softmax cannot produce exact zero probability, and therefore cannot rule out non-canonical sequences, holds only for an unstructured softmax model — finite-state constraints, masking, or constrained decoding can pin non-canonical sequence probability to exactly zero.

### 1.4 Computational scope: finite, but not necessarily tractable

For a deterministic, non-erasing, multiplicative decoder with a trivial kernel over a finite vocabulary, every token outputs at least one character, so any token string decoding to a text sigma has no more tokens than sigma has characters, giving a finite (but potentially exponentially large) preimage set, roughly bounded by the sum over lengths i from 1 to the length of sigma of the vocabulary size to the power i. Multiplicativity implies bounded variation, but concluding finite-state realizability additionally needs a rational-set-preserving assumption the paper states explicitly — bounded variation alone is not sufficient. **One boundary case the paper's stated bound misses**: the empty string. Multiplicativity forces the empty token string to decode to the empty character string, and the trivial-kernel assumption rules out any non-empty token string decoding to the empty text, so the preimage of the empty text is exactly the singleton set containing the empty token string — but the stated bound, starting its sum at length 1, evaluates to zero when the text has length zero, so it needs to be restated to include length zero or handled as a separate case. Special tokens that decode to the empty string (control markers, some BOS/EOS handling) violate the trivial-kernel assumption outright, and repeatedly inserting them can give a single text infinitely many preimages.

**Section 5's computational results are, despite the paper's general stochastic-map setup, effectively restricted to deterministic decoders.** Sections 3 allows both tau and kappa to be stochastic; Section 5's multiplicativity, kernel, preimage, bounded-variation, and subsequentiality results all treat kappa(delta) as a single fixed string, so they only cover deterministic decoder functions. Extending them to genuinely stochastic decoders would require redefining stochastic transduction, support relations, and summation complexity — the current proofs do not carry over automatically.

### 1.5 A derived, finite-sample error bound

The round-trip kernel's contraction property under total variation immediately gives a useful, non-asymptotic decomposition that the paper's own theorem states only as an iff condition but that is directly usable in practice:

the total-variation distance between (kappa applied to q_n) and p-star is at most the sum of (the distance between q_n and q-star) and (the distance between K applied to p-star and p-star).

The first term is the token-level language model's own estimation error; the second is the tokenizer's **irreducible round-trip bias**. An exact tokenizer makes the second term exactly zero; a non-exact tokenizer leaves this term in place no matter how good the token-level model becomes. This decomposition is not stated verbatim in the paper, but follows immediately from its argument, and is more useful for citing purposes than the bare iff statement, since it separates model error from tokenizer error into two independently estimable pieces.

### 1.6 What this paper does and does not establish

Its central theorem is correct but conditional in an important way: it assumes token-level consistency has *already been achieved* and only characterizes the residual bias left after decoding — it identifies where decoding *would* land if the token model has already converged, but does not show that a real neural language model actually achieves that convergence. It does not compare tokenizers by sample complexity, model capacity, optimization difficulty, sequence length, or inductive bias — all of that is folded into the black-box assumption "q_n has already converged." So it answers "if the token model eventually learns correctly, where does decoding end up," not "which tokenizer makes it easier for a model to learn correctly."

**Retained conclusion for this project**: a tokenizer is a stochastic encoder/decoder pair, the correct consistency test is distribution-relative (K applied to p-star equals p-star), universal preservation (K equals the identity) is the stronger notion called exactness, stochastic tokenization is compatible with exactness exactly when different texts' segmentations have disjoint support, and canonical token-sequence probability is generally not character-string probability — the latter needs marginalizing over the whole decoder fiber. This is precisely the algebraic backbone behind the "already-proven case" and the "section property" discussed at length in the token theory's companion notes.

## 2. Zouhar, Meister, et al., "Tokenization and the Noiseless Channel" (arXiv:2306.16842)

### 2.1 The formal setup

This paper restricts attention to invertible, deterministic tokenizers and interprets tokenization as a dictionary code sent through a noiseless channel: the tokenizer induces a unigram distribution p_Delta over token types, and that distribution is treated as if it were the source distribution behind an optimal prefix code. Renyi entropy is introduced as the coding quantity naturally associated with a *discounted* expected code length (as opposed to Shannon entropy's association with plain expected code length), and **Renyi efficiency**, roughly the ratio H_alpha divided by H_0, is defined and bounded as an intrinsic score of how balanced the induced token-frequency distribution is, normalized against a uniform-code baseline. The correct relation between the Renyi order alpha and the coding-cost discount parameter s is s equals the inverse of alpha minus 1; the paper's text has a sign error at one point, stating the relation with a plus sign instead of a minus sign, inconsistent with both its own preceding identity and its own Theorem 4.2 — a slip worth flagging for anyone citing the derivation directly, though it does not affect the final metric.

The claim that this efficiency score *predicts downstream model quality* — the "Compression Principle" — is explicitly labeled a **hypothesis** by the paper itself, not a theorem. The coding theorems establish the entropy-to-code-cost relationship; they do not, and are not claimed to, establish the leap from coding efficiency to neural learnability.

### 2.2 The empirical result, and its limits

At a tuned Renyi order of alpha = 2.5, the efficiency score correlates with machine-translation BLEU at 0.78 — a substantial correlation. But several qualifications matter. The reported experiments approximate the theoretical expression by setting a covariance term to zero, and while the paper reports this term is empirically small and negative, the reported metric remains an approximation to the theoretically derived bound rather than the bound itself. The order alpha = 2.5 is chosen by grid search and is explicitly flagged by the paper's own Compression Principle as model-dependent — it should not be treated as a universal tokenizer constant, transplanted unexamined to a different task or language pair. **Most importantly for how much weight this metric should carry: tokenizer identity provides predictive information orthogonal to Renyi efficiency** — meaning the metric, however useful, is demonstrably incomplete as a full account of what makes a tokenizer good. And the empirical evidence is drawn from essentially one language pair, one dataset scale, and one model family; the paper itself lists language direction, model choice, and training data as possible hidden confounds it has not ruled out.

### 2.3 What the metric actually measures

H_alpha divided by H_0 measures effective support relative to the full vocabulary support. For alpha greater than 1, Renyi entropy is dominated by high-probability tokens, so a low score specifically exposes distributions whose mass is concentrated in a handful of extremely frequent tokens. **The observed positive association with BLEU supports the narrower claim "avoid excessive concentration of mass in a few very frequent tokens," not the broader claim that compression by itself causes learnability** — a distinction the paper's own framing sometimes blurs but that matters for how the result should be cited.

### 2.4 Assessment

Treat Renyi efficiency as an empirically promising, cheap-to-compute frequency-balance diagnostic — useful because it can be checked before training any downstream model at all — and not as a proven optimality criterion. The source-coding theory explains what the metric *means* as a statement about the induced unigram distribution; it does not, and cannot on its own, establish the metric's relationship to downstream learnability, which remains task-conditioned and correlational. Note the relation to the previous paper: Gastaldi et al. ask whether the encoder-decoder round trip *preserves the target distribution at all*; this paper assumes an invertible, lossless tokenizer as a starting premise and asks a different, downstream question — whether the resulting token-frequency distribution happens to be a *favorable* one to learn from. Consistency is a correctness condition; Renyi efficiency is a proposed quality predictor layered on top of an already-consistent tokenizer.

## 3. Vieira, Cotterell, et al., "On the Proper Treatment of Tokenization in Psycholinguistics" (arXiv:2410.02691)

### 3.1 The problem this paper solves

Psycholinguistic experiments define regions of interest and focal areas — the first three characters of a word, a look-ahead window, a fixation region — as character-level objects, entirely independent of whatever tokenizer a given language model happens to use. But a modern LM defines probabilities over *token* strings, not character strings, and an arbitrary psycholinguistic substring generally does not align with any token boundary at all. The paper's central methodological claim: **once the target quantity (surprisal over some character region) is properly formulated as a character-string probability, tokenization becomes irrelevant to the theory** — but only after paying the cost of correctly computing that character-string probability, which the tokenizer's internal token-string probabilities do not directly give you.

### 3.2 The formal machinery, and its relation to the foundations paper

Let p_Delta be a token-string language model with a deterministic decoder kappa. The induced character-string probability is the decoder's pushforward: p_Sigma(sigma) equals the sum, over every token string delta with kappa(delta) equal to sigma, of p_Delta(delta). Character-prefix probabilities are computed the same way, summing over every token string whose decoded output covers the prefix; a focal-area surprisal is then a character-level conditional probability formed from the ratio of the relevant prefix probabilities, making the predictor invariant to whether the chosen focal area happens to land on a token boundary or not.

This paper adopts, as a working assumption, exactly the special case isolated as practically important by the foundations paper above: kappa(tau(sigma)) equals sigma (encoding then decoding returns the original text), while tau(kappa(delta)) equals delta is **not** assumed to hold outside the canonical encoder image. The population of non-canonical tokenizations sitting outside that canonical image is precisely the source of the decoder-fiber ambiguity this paper's marginalization procedure has to sum over — this paper's central computational step is a direct, concrete instantiation of the foundations paper's abstract decoder-fiber principle, applied specifically to psycholinguistic surprisal estimation.

### 3.3 Tractability and empirical setup

Exact marginalization over every token string compatible with a given character prefix can, in general, be **#P-hard** for non-canonical tokenizations — a genuinely hard combinatorial problem, not merely an inconvenient sum. The experiments approximate this marginalization using beam summation with a beam size of 5, throughout, on GPT-2 small only. This approximation's error is not propagated into the reported confidence intervals, nor is it compared across the different focal-area lengths tested — a real limitation on how precisely the empirical rankings below should be trusted, distinct from the methodological correction itself, which holds independently of the approximation quality.

### 3.4 Empirical findings

On the CELER eye-tracking dataset, a focal area consisting of just the **first three characters** is motivated as the information plausibly available to a reader before a skip decision is made, and its surprisal turns out to be the **strongest predictor of skip rate** among the twenty regions and focal-area constructions tested via cross-validated regression — while full-region surprisal (the conventional choice) is the *weakest* predictor tested, with roughly half the incremental R-squared of the first-three-character predictor. Look-ahead and prefix-based focal areas match or improve on full-region surprisal across several other reading-time measures as well.

**This empirical finding is theoretically coherent, not merely a curiosity of the approximation**: the decision to skip a word must be made before the reader's eyes fixate on that region, so a full-region surprisal predictor is smuggling in information the reader could not plausibly have had access to at decision time. Look-ahead improvements likewise point to genuine parafoveal or integration effects in reading. **These findings are stronger evidence than a mere tokenizer artifact being corrected** — they show that choosing the linguistically and temporally appropriate character window, rather than whatever window happens to be convenient given a particular tokenizer's boundaries, changes the substantive psycholinguistic conclusions drawn from the data.

### 3.5 Boundaries

The tokenizer is assumed deterministic, exact, and multiplicative; stochastic tokenizers and lossy normalization schemes would require a more general stochastic-decoder treatment not covered here. All empirical results use GPT-2 small — a different model may distribute probability mass across non-canonical tokenizations differently, and could shift the focal-area rankings. The empirical study is restricted to English L1 eye-tracking data with measurements averaged across participants, removing individual variation that could plausibly interact with perceptual span and the optimal focal-area choice; other languages, self-paced reading paradigms, and participant-level mixed-effects modeling are not tested. The paper's slogan that "tokenization is irrelevant" is true at the level of the theoretical target quantity, but it is true only *after* a computationally non-trivial marginalization step whose own approximation quality still matters for the empirical conclusions drawn from it.

### 3.6 How this paper relates to the other two

Where the foundations paper (arXiv:2407.11606) supplies the general stochastic-map language and explains, in the abstract, why decoder fibers must be marginalized over, this paper is a direct instantiation of that principle for one specific, practically important downstream use: psycholinguistic surprisal estimation. Where the noiseless-channel paper (arXiv:2306.16842) asks whether the induced token distribution is efficient to *learn from*, this paper instead removes tokenization from the definition of the linguistic quantity being *evaluated* in the first place — a different question entirely, aimed at correctness of measurement rather than efficiency of training.

## 4. What the three papers establish together

Read as a stack, the three papers answer three different questions about the same underlying object — the tokenizer as an encoder/decoder pair between characters and tokens — and none of the three answers the others' question.

The foundations paper gives the algebra: a tokenizer is a pair of stochastic maps, and whether the resulting model preserves the true character-level distribution reduces to a fixed-point condition on the round-trip kernel K = kappa composed with tau. This is a statement about **correctness** — does the round trip lose or distort information at the level of the whole distribution — and it is the formal ancestor of the "already-proven case" (the lossless round trip) and the "section property" (whether an anchor returns to its own class) used throughout the companion token-theory notes.

The noiseless-channel paper assumes correctness (an invertible, exact tokenizer) and asks a *different* question: is the token-frequency distribution that results a *good* one to learn from, independent of whether it is lossless. This is a statement about **learnability**, layered on top of an assumed-correct encoding, and its central metric (Renyi efficiency) is explicitly flagged by its own authors as a promising but incomplete, model-dependent, correlational diagnostic — not a proof that any particular compression target causes better learning.

The psycholinguistics paper assumes the general correctness framework from the first paper and applies it to show that a downstream consumer of a token language model (a psycholinguistic surprisal estimate) must marginalize over the tokenizer's own internal ambiguity to recover the character-level quantity it actually wants — and that failing to do so, by naively using token-aligned regions of interest, changes the empirical conclusions drawn from real eye-tracking data, not merely their precision.

**The throughline for the token theory this note supports**: "does the round trip preserve information" (correctness), "is the resulting discrete representation efficient to learn from" (learnability), and "how do you correctly compute quantities the tokenizer was never designed to expose" (downstream marginalization) are three genuinely separate questions, formally distinguishable, and conflating any two of them — as informal discussion of tokenization routinely does — obscures which of the three is actually being debated in any given claim about why a tokenizer is or is not "good."
